## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Research Question

Did the COVID-19 pandemic exert a statistically detectable, directionally consistent, and economically meaningful causal influence on financial market indicators — and if so, how did that influence evolve across the distinct phases of the pandemic's economic arc? This project provides a systematic, quantitative answer using econometric time series methods applied to daily pandemic data and daily financial market data across the full 966-day dataset, from the first recorded U.S. case on January 22, 2020, through September 14, 2022.

The motivating recognition is that "COVID affected markets" is too coarse a claim to be analytically useful. It is simultaneously almost certainly true in a general sense and almost entirely uninformative about the specific mechanisms, magnitudes, timing, and indicator classes through which the pandemic's economic footprint operated. Precise and falsifiable questions are necessary to generate findings with genuine explanatory content. Which COVID metric carried the stronger causal signal — confirmed case counts, confirmed deaths, or the combined total — and did the answer change as the pandemic's epidemiological properties evolved? Which economic indicators were most reliably predicted by pandemic data, and through which transmission mechanisms? Did the causal relationship persist across the full pandemic timeline, or was it concentrated in specific phases and attenuated or absent in others? Did the form of the relationship — its sign, lag structure, magnitude, and causal direction — change as the pandemic transitioned from acute novel shock to endemic background condition? And what does the pattern of those changes reveal about how financial markets incorporate genuinely unprecedented information across time?

These questions resist answers that treat the pandemic as a single undifferentiated event. A project that analyzes COVID's economic impact across the full 966-day window without phase segmentation will average the Initial Shock's acute, sentiment-driven, equity-dominant causal signal against the Adaptation period's slower-moving, supply-disruption-driven, commodity-dominant signal and the Recovery phase's lagged, lockdown-mediated, currency-persistent signal — producing composite statistics that accurately describe none of the three and obscure the structured sequence of mechanism transitions that is the project's central substantive finding. The phase-segmented design, combined with systematic variation of COVID variables, transformations, and indicator categories across notebooks, was chosen precisely to make that sequence visible rather than averaged away.

The answer this project provides to its central research question is neither a simple yes nor a qualified maybe. It is a structured yes with a specific architecture: COVID-19 exerted genuine, directionally asymmetric, statistically robust causal pressure on financial markets across all three pandemic phases, operating through mechanisms that changed in type, speed, magnitude, and indicator class reach in a specific order that maps directly onto the pandemic's evolving epidemiological and policy environment. That structured yes — with its phase-specific causal mechanisms, its indicator class rotation, its COVID variable shifts, and its composite score decay arc — is what the project's six-component analytical framework was designed to recover, and what the findings documented across the phase analyses collectively establish.

### 1.2 Data and Scope

The dataset spans 966 calendar days from January 22, 2020, through September 14, 2022, covering the complete arc of the COVID-19 pandemic's acute economic impact — from the first recorded U.S. case through the Federal Reserve's aggressive tightening cycle and the beginning of post-pandemic normalization. All series are observed at daily frequency. Three COVID-19 data variants are tested against six categories of economic indicators, with each COVID variant available in multiple transformed representations, producing a combinatorially large space of candidate pairings that the composite scoring framework systematically evaluates and ranks.

**COVID-19 Variables (X)**

| Variable | Description |
|---|---|
| `covid_cases` | Confirmed COVID-19 cases |
| `covid_deaths` | Confirmed COVID-19 deaths |
| `covid_c&d` | Total confirmed cases and deaths |

Each COVID variable is tested in its raw daily form and with up to three optional transformations applied independently or in combination: a 7-day rolling average (`x_roll`), which suppresses within-week reporting periodicity and smooths daily noise; cumulative accumulation (`xcml`), which converts daily counts into running totals that proxy for the pandemic's accumulated economic damage; and percentage change (`xpct`), which transforms the series into stationary growth rates suitable for direct comparison with financial market return series. The interaction of three base variables with up to three binary transformation flags generates a rich space of COVID signal representations whose differential performance across phases — which transformations dominate which indicator classes in which periods — is itself a substantive finding about how financial markets processed pandemic information at different points in the pandemic's evolution.

**Economic Indicators (Y)**

| Category | Instruments | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `stk_mkt` | 5 indices | Major U.S. stock market indices |
| `metals` | 5 metals | Industrial and precious metal commodity prices |
| `exchg_rts` | 5 pairs | Major currency / USD bilateral exchange rates |
| `agr1` / `agr2` | 5 indices each | Agricultural commodity price indices |
| `intrt_rts` | 5 instruments | Interest rate instruments across the yield curve |
| `petrol_plus` | 5 indices | Oil, gas, and broader energy sector indices |

The six indicator categories were selected to span the primary transmission channels through which pandemic severity could plausibly reach financial market prices: investor sentiment and earnings expectations through equity indices; physical supply chain disruption through metal and agricultural commodity prices; monetary policy divergence and safe-haven dynamics through exchange rates; yield curve and credit market responses through interest rate instruments; and energy demand and supply disruption through petroleum and energy indices. The five-instrument depth within each category provides cross-sectional variation that distinguishes between instruments within the same asset class — allowing the analysis to identify, for example, which specific metals were most COVID-sensitive and why — rather than treating each category as a single homogeneous exposure.

The full combinatorial space of COVID variables, transformations, indicator categories, and analytical periods generates several thousand candidate pairings, all of which are scored by the composite framework in the candidate selection notebook before phase-specific analyses are conducted on the highest-ranked pairings. This exhaustive scoring approach — evaluating all combinations rather than selecting on the basis of prior conviction or observed correlation — is the design feature that allows the project's cross-phase variable and indicator rotation findings to be interpreted as data-driven rather than assumption-driven: the framework recovered which COVID metrics, transformations, and indicator classes were most causally connected in each phase without being told in advance where to look.

### 1.3 Pandemic Phase Definitions

The 966-day dataset is divided into four analytically distinct periods whose boundaries reflect genuine structural transitions in the pandemic's macroeconomic character — points at which the dominant transmission mechanism, the operative policy environment, and the informational structure of COVID data all shifted simultaneously rather than gradually.

| Phase | Start | End | Duration | Macroeconomic Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Full Period** | 01/22/2020 | 09/14/2022 | ~966 days | Complete dataset; all three phases pooled |
| **Initial Shock** | 01/22/2020 | 06/07/2020 | ~137 days | Pandemic emergence, historic market crash, emergency policy response |
| **Adaptation** | 06/08/2020 | 11/25/2021 | ~536 days | Vaccine development and rollout, market recovery, Delta wave |
| **Recovery** | 11/26/2021 | 09/14/2022 | ~292 days | Omicron wave, Federal Reserve tightening cycle, post-pandemic normalization |

The Initial Shock phase spans the 137 days from the first confirmed U.S. case through early June 2020 — encompassing the full arc from pandemic emergence through the fastest equity market crash in modern financial history, the Federal Reserve's two emergency rate cuts totaling 150 basis points outside its normal meeting schedule, the launch of unlimited quantitative easing and an unprecedented suite of emergency lending facilities, and the passage of the $2.2 trillion CARES Act. This phase is defined by a condition that was historically bounded and analytically unique: genuine, acute informational asymmetry between what the pandemic would ultimately prove to be and what market participants could know about it in real time. COVID data during this phase carried maximum causal leverage over asset prices precisely because markets had no established pricing framework for a novel pandemic of unknown severity, unknown duration, and unknown policy response.

The Adaptation phase spans 536 days from June 2020 through late November 2021 — the longest phase by duration and the most structurally complex by the diversity of economic forces operating simultaneously within it. The phase encompasses the initial economic reopening and partial recovery of summer 2020, the second and third mortality waves of winter 2020–21, the Emergency Use Authorization of the first COVID vaccines in December 2020 and the subsequent rollout through spring 2021, the Delta variant resurgence of summer and fall 2021, and the sustained commodity super cycle driven by pandemic-era fiscal stimulus and supply chain disruption. It is defined by the transition from COVID as the dominant and largely exclusive market-organizing force to COVID as one significant factor among many competing for the same financial market pricing bandwidth — a transition that reorganized the dominant transmission mechanism from equity risk sentiment to commodity supply disruption and shifted the most informative COVID metric from the combined cases-and-deaths series to deaths alone.

The Recovery phase spans 292 days from late November 2021 through September 14, 2022, opening almost exactly at the moment the Omicron variant achieved global dominance and closing as Federal Reserve tightening and post-pandemic normalization had substantially reshaped the macroeconomic environment. It is defined not by the absence of pandemic influence but by the presence of three additional macro forces of comparable or greater magnitude operating simultaneously — the Omicron wave's case-mortality decoupling, the Federal Reserve's fastest rate hike cycle since the 1980s, and the Russia-Ukraine war's acute commodity supply shock — that competed with the COVID signal for the same transmission channels and compressed the pandemic's detectable causal footprint into longer lags and more episodic activation patterns than prior phases had produced.

The Full Period analysis pools all three phases into a single 966-day regression, intentionally trading sub-period specificity for sample size and temporal comprehensiveness. It is not a fourth pandemic phase but a distinct analytical lens: one positioned to detect relationships that were too slow-moving or too diffusely distributed across the pandemic's timeline to dominate any single-phase window, and to identify which indicator classes and COVID variables proved most persistent across the complete pandemic arc rather than most acute during any specific sub-period. The full-period and phase analyses are designed as complements rather than alternatives — each revealing dimensions of the COVID → financial market causal structure that the other's temporal scope cannot capture.

### 1.4 Analytical Methodology

Every COVID–indicator pairing is analyzed through a standardized six-component pipeline implemented in Python using the custom `mathx` library. The pipeline is applied identically across all pairings, phases, and notebooks — ensuring that cross-phase and cross-indicator comparisons reflect genuine differences in the underlying causal relationships rather than methodological inconsistencies in how those relationships were tested. Each component addresses a distinct dimension of the causal question, and the composite scoring framework weights their outputs into a single comparable metric only after all six have been computed independently.

1. **Stationarity Testing and Structural Break Detection.** Before any causal analysis is performed, all series are subjected to a comprehensive stationarity diagnostic pipeline combining the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test, the Kwiatkowski-Phillips-Schmidt-Shin test, the Zivot-Andrews structural break test, and Bai-Perron multiple breakpoint detection. The ADF and KPSS tests are interpreted jointly through a four-verdict framework — `stationary`, `non_stationary`, `trend_stationary`, and `inconclusive` — whose specific combination determines the appropriate stationarity correction for each series. Structural break detection precedes transformation: identifying break dates before differencing or percentage-change transformation ensures that the correction is calibrated to the actual data-generating process of each series rather than to the idealized processes that standard unit root tests presuppose. For the COVID series, which are non-stationary by construction and subject to sharp regime changes at wave onset and variant emergence, this pre-testing step is essential for preventing miscalibrated stationarity corrections from contaminating the downstream causal results.

2. **Polynomial Degree Optimization and Correlation.** The contemporaneous relationship between the COVID variable and each economic indicator is characterized through a two-stage process. First, AIC-minimizing polynomial degree selection with cross-validated R² evaluation and overfitting detection identifies the functional form that best captures the scatter plot geometry — distinguishing between linear, quadratic, higher-order, and structureless relationships without imposing a functional form in advance. Second, Pearson or Spearman correlation is applied depending on the distributional properties of the series pair: Spearman rank-order correlation is used when either series exhibits significant departures from bivariate normality, heavy tails, or the right-skewed distributions characteristic of pandemic count data, while Pearson is applied when distributional assumptions are satisfied. The cross-validated R² is the operative diagnostic for interpreting polynomial fits: negative values indicate models that generalize worse than a naive mean prediction, constituting a stronger null result than a near-zero positive R² and serving as the primary indicator of absent contemporaneous structure.

3. **Granger Causality Testing.** The directional asymmetry between the COVID → indicator and indicator → COVID directions is the project's primary causal identification test. Bidirectional Granger causality is assessed using the optimal method — F-test or chi-squared — selected by the distributional properties of the series, at the optimal lag chosen by information criteria. Four test statistics are computed for each direction at each lag: the F-statistic, chi-squared statistic, likelihood ratio test, and params-F test. Results requiring all four statistics to agree before a `high confidence` conclusion is recorded provide a conservative and multi-method-robust causal identification standard that single-statistic implementations cannot achieve. The `conclusion` field distinguishes between `high confidence` causality, `high confidence` non-causality, and flagged results such as `non-normal residuals or insufficient sample size` that require interpretive qualification. A finding of COVID → indicator significance without indicator → COVID significance constitutes the directional asymmetry that the project treats as evidence of genuine causal precedence rather than contemporaneous co-movement driven by a common underlying shock.

4. **VAR/VECM Dynamic Modeling.** The `mathx.fit_var_or_vecm()` function automatically selects between a Vector Autoregression estimated in differences and a Vector Error Correction Model based on cointegration status confirmed by trace and max-eigenvalue tests. When cointegration is confirmed, the VECM specification imposes the long-run equilibrium structure that the cointegrating relationship implies and allows the estimation of error correction speeds that measure how rapidly each series adjusts toward the long-run equilibrium following a deviation. Impulse Response Functions quantify the magnitude, sign, and persistence of COVID shocks on economic indicators across a 30-period horizon, distinguishing between transient impulses that decay rapidly and persistent displacements that accumulate without mean reversion. Forecast Error Variance Decomposition measures the fraction of each indicator's total forecast error variance attributable to COVID shocks at each horizon — the most direct quantitative measure of COVID's relative importance as a driver of each indicator's dynamics — and provides the basis for comparing COVID's explanatory power across indicator classes, phases, and model specifications on a common percentage scale.
   
5. **Rolling Correlation Analysis:** The time-varying character of the COVID–indicator relationship within each phase is analyzed through rolling Spearman correlations computed at optimally selected window lengths and minimum period requirements. Window and minimum period selection uses a cross-validation scoring procedure that balances correlation stability, significance rate, NaN fraction, and autocorrelation penalty into a single score for each candidate specification — ensuring that the rolling window parameters reflect the informational properties of the specific series pair rather than an arbitrary conventional choice. Rolling correlations that sustain a directional sign across many consecutive windows indicate a persistent causal channel; rapid sign-switching with near-zero means indicates episodic or absent co-movement; and the specific timing of elevated rolling correlation periods — whether they align with pandemic wave peaks, policy announcement windows, or supply disruption events — provides sub-period evidence about the specific conditions under which the COVID–indicator relationship was active versus dormant.

6. **Lag Correlation Analysis:** The temporal ordering of the COVID–indicator relationship is quantified through Spearman cross-correlations computed across a ±24-day lag window, with Bonferroni correction applied for the multiple simultaneous comparisons across all tested lags. A peak lag in the positive region — where the COVID series at time t is most strongly correlated with the indicator at time t+k for positive k — confirms that pandemic data anticipated indicator movements rather than merely synchronizing with them contemporaneously or following them. The Bonferroni-corrected p-value at the peak lag provides the significance assessment; the r_improvement metric — the difference between the peak lag correlation and the same-day correlation — quantifies how much temporal structure the lag dimension adds beyond the contemporaneous relationship. The lag correlation is interpreted jointly with the Granger causality results: when both confirm the same directional leading structure, the evidence for COVID information preceding market movements is strengthened by cross-method convergence; when they diverge — as when Granger significance at short lags coexists with a displaced bivariate lag peak driven by autocorrelation confounding — the Granger result is treated as the more reliable directional indicator and the divergence is interpreted through the methodological differences between the two approaches.

### 1.5 Composite Scoring Framework

To rank COVID–indicator pairings systematically across the full combinatorial space of COVID variants, transformations, indicator categories, and analytical periods, a weighted composite score aggregates the outputs of the six analytical components into a single comparable value on a scale from 0 to 100.

| Component | Weight | What It Captures |
|---|---|---|
| Granger Causality | **0.30** | Directional predictive asymmetry and multi-test statistical significance |
| VAR/VECM Dynamics | **0.25** | IRF peak magnitude and sign, FEVD X-share, error correction stability |
| Cointegration | **0.20** | Long-run equilibrium relationship confirmed by trace and max-eigenvalue tests |
| Lag Correlation | **0.15** | Peak delayed association and X-leads-Y temporal ordering |
| Contemporaneous Correlation | **0.10** | Same-day association strength and polynomial fit quality |

The weight distribution reflects a deliberate ordering of evidential standards. Granger causality receives the highest weight because directional predictive asymmetry — the demonstration that COVID data predicts indicator movements beyond what those indicators' own histories explain, without the reverse being equally true — is the most demanding and most directly causal of the five criteria. A statistically significant Granger result that survives four simultaneous test statistics and shows clean asymmetry between directions cannot easily be attributed to shared trend structure, contemporaneous co-movement, or common underlying shocks in the way that correlation-based findings can. VAR/VECM dynamics receive the second-highest weight because impulse response functions and forecast error variance decompositions quantify the magnitude and persistence of the causal relationship in ways that binary significance thresholds cannot — a pairing where COVID shocks explain 40% of an indicator's forecast variance is fundamentally different from one where COVID shocks explain 4%, even if both clear the same Granger significance threshold. Cointegration receives substantial weight because a confirmed long-run equilibrium relationship between a COVID series and a financial indicator — validated by both trace and max-eigenvalue tests — establishes that the two series are bound by a stable attractor rather than merely trending together spuriously over the sample window. Lag correlation and contemporaneous correlation receive lower weights not because they are uninformative but because they are more susceptible to confounding by autocorrelation, shared trend structure, and phase-mixing dynamics that the higher-weighted components are specifically designed to control for.

Higher composite scores indicate relationships that are stronger, more directionally consistent, more persistent, and more methodologically robust across multiple independent analytical dimensions simultaneously. A high score that rests on convergent evidence from Granger causality, VECM dynamics, and cointegration — as the Initial Shock equity results do — carries fundamentally different evidential weight than a high score driven primarily by contemporaneous correlation or a single analytical component. The cross-component convergence pattern within each phase analysis is therefore as analytically important as the composite score itself, and the phase-level discussions document where convergence is achieved and where individual components diverge from the composite conclusion.

The scoring is computed separately for levels specifications and percentage-change specifications, producing two independent rankings for each phase and period. This dual-framework design serves a specific inferential purpose: levels and percentage-change analyses ask structurally different questions — whether pandemic trajectory co-moves with indicator trajectory in a cointegrating long-run sense, versus whether pandemic data acceleration predicts indicator return direction in a short-run predictive sense — and the pairings that lead each framework are not necessarily the same. Where both frameworks agree in identifying the same pairing as dominant, the convergence constitutes the strongest possible evidence that a genuine causal relationship exists at both the trajectory level and the return level simultaneously. Where the frameworks diverge, the pattern of disagreement is itself analytically informative: levels significance without percentage-change significance suggests a long-run co-trending relationship that does not extend to short-run return predictability, while percentage-change significance without levels significance suggests an episodic short-run causal channel without a stable long-run equilibrium. Both patterns appear in the project's results across phases and indicator categories, and both are interpreted as substantively meaningful rather than as methodological inconsistencies to be resolved by selecting one framework over the other.

### 2.1: Levels Analysis: Methodology and Interpretive Framework

The levels analysis tests whether COVID data — expressed as cumulative counts or rolling-average transforms of those counts — synchronizes with and causally precedes economic indicator levels over the course of each phase. Both the COVID series and the economic indicator series are non-stationary in levels: they trend over time rather than fluctuating around a fixed mean, and their statistical properties change across the sample window. This shared non-stationarity is not simply a technical nuisance to be corrected away — it is economically meaningful. During an acute pandemic phase, cumulative case and death counts trend monotonically upward by construction, and many financial indicators trend simultaneously in response to the same underlying shock. The co-movement of two trending series can reflect either a genuine long-run equilibrium relationship (cointegration) or a spurious correlation driven by shared trend structure with no causal content. Distinguishing between these two possibilities is the central inferential challenge of the levels analysis.

The stationarity correction applied throughout — ADF and KPSS testing followed by differencing or percentage-change transformation as appropriate — addresses this challenge directly, but the levels analysis retains more of the original trend structure than the percentage-change framework does. Rather than asking whether short-run accelerations in COVID data predict short-run changes in economic indicators, the levels analysis asks whether the long-run trajectory of COVID data and the long-run trajectory of economic indicators move together in a statistically disciplined way. This is a more relaxed evidentiary standard in one sense: shared trending behavior is easier to detect than short-run predictive content, and the Granger causality tests in the levels framework are more likely to find significance when both series are driven by a common underlying force — the pandemic itself — that operates on both simultaneously. It is a more demanding standard in another sense: the cointegration tests required to validate the VECM specification impose a specific equilibrium structure on the relationship, requiring not just that the series trend together but that they are bound by a stable long-run attractor from which deviations are mean-reverting.

When cointegration is confirmed, the VECM framework is appropriate and the Granger causality, IRF, and FEVD results carry a well-defined economic interpretation: the COVID series and the economic indicator share a long-run equilibrium, and deviations from that equilibrium generate error-correction dynamics in one or both series. In this case, finding that COVID data Granger-causes an economic indicator in the levels framework constitutes evidence of a genuine long-run causal relationship — the pandemic trajectory systematically shaped the trajectory of the economic variable over the phase, not merely co-moved with it by accident. When cointegration is not confirmed but both series are non-stationary, the risk of spurious correlation is elevated, and the Granger causality results must be interpreted with greater caution: statistical significance may reflect shared trend structure rather than a causal mechanism with economic content.

The levels analysis is therefore best understood as complementary to rather than redundant with the percentage-change analysis. Where both frameworks agree — finding significant COVID → indicator causality in both levels and percentage-change specifications — the evidence for a genuine causal relationship is substantially strengthened, as it holds across both the long-run trajectory and the short-run acceleration dimensions of the data. Where the frameworks diverge — finding significance in levels but not in percentage changes, or vice versa — the pattern of disagreement is itself analytically informative. Significance in levels but not in percentage changes suggests a long-run co-trending relationship that does not extend to short-run predictive content, consistent with habituation or shared macro drivers rather than active causal transmission. Significance in percentage changes but not in levels suggests an episodic short-run causal channel that operates during specific sub-periods without producing a stable long-run equilibrium — the pattern most consistent with the wave-driven, episodic COVID signal observed in the Adaptation phase rolling correlations.

The choice between levels and percentage-change frameworks is not arbitrary but follows directly from the stationarity properties of the series in each phase and the economic question being asked. For the Initial Shock, where both COVID data and equity indices are non-stationary and the 137-day window is short enough that shared trend structure is difficult to disentangle from genuine cointegration, the levels analysis provides evidence about the joint trending behavior of the series during the crash-and-recovery arc. For the Adaptation period, where the percentage-change framework strips out the long-run trend to focus on short-run predictive content, the levels analysis serves as a check on whether the causal relationships identified in percentage changes also hold at the trajectory level — and whether the cointegrating relationships identified by the VECM specifications represent stable long-run equilibria or artifacts of the extended 536-day window's structural heterogeneity.

**Full Period Levels Analysis: Leading Relationships.** The `covid_deaths` → `agr1` pairing leads the Full Period levels analysis with a composite score of 48.74, establishing agricultural commodities as the most persistently COVID-sensitive indicator category across the entire 966-day analytical window. This result is worth unpacking carefully, because it is not the relationship that the Initial Shock or Adaptation analyses would have predicted as the dominant long-run pairing.
The absence of a transformation advantage — neither the cumulative nor rolling-average transform of the COVID deaths series dominates the raw series in this pairing — is itself informative. During shorter phase windows, transformation choices matter considerably: the 7-day rolling average smooths out reporting noise, and the cumulative transform captures the pandemic's accumulated severity rather than its daily fluctuation. At the 966-day full-period frequency, however, these distinctions collapse. The raw COVID deaths series already embodies a long, multi-wave trajectory that is structurally smooth at the scale of months, and its cumulative and rolling transforms become increasingly collinear with the raw series as the window lengthens. The finding that no transformation dominates therefore suggests that the causal signal between COVID mortality and agricultural commodity prices operated at the level of the pandemic's broad trajectory — its waves, plateaus, and secular decline — rather than at the level of short-run acceleration episodes that transformations are designed to isolate. This is a long-run relationship in the fullest sense: the multi-year arc of the pandemic shaped the multi-year arc of agricultural commodity prices through mechanisms that operated continuously rather than episodically.

The economic mechanism connecting COVID mortality to agricultural commodity prices across the full period is more structurally coherent than it might initially appear. Agricultural supply chains are among the most labor-intensive in the global economy and among the least substitutable for automation on short timescales. Harvesting, processing, packing, and distribution operations depend on geographically concentrated workforces that proved acutely vulnerable to COVID transmission throughout the pandemic, from the meatpacking plant outbreaks of spring 2020 through the agricultural labor disruptions that persisted across the Delta and subsequent variant waves. At the same time, agricultural demand was restructured by the pandemic — away from food service and hospitality channels toward retail and stockpiling — in ways that created supply-demand mismatches at the commodity level even when aggregate consumption was unchanged. Shipping disruption, itself a persistent feature of the pandemic economy, constrained the movement of both agricultural inputs (fertilizers, chemicals) and outputs (grains, oilseeds) across global markets. And the monetary accommodation that accompanied the pandemic's entire duration provided a sustained inflationary tailwind to commodity prices broadly. The COVID deaths series, as a proxy for pandemic severity across all four of these channels simultaneously, captures the full-period trajectory of this complex of forces in a way that shorter-window, higher-frequency analyses necessarily miss.

The broader commodity dominance of the top-10 rankings reinforces this interpretation. Agricultural commodities (`agr1`) and oil and gas (`petrol_plus`) together account for 9 of the 10 top positions in the Full Period levels analysis, with equity indices — which dominated the Initial Shock — largely absent from the upper rankings. This reordering reflects the fundamental shift in COVID's economic transmission mechanism across the pandemic's full duration. In the acute phase, COVID operated through financial markets via risk sentiment, uncertainty, and demand destruction — channels that affect equities directly and immediately. Across the full 966-day window, these acute channels were transient; what persisted was the pandemic's structural footprint on physical commodity markets through supply disruption, logistical constraint, and the sustained stimulus response that commodity-producing economies used to offset pandemic damage. The Full Period levels analysis is therefore not simply the average of the phase analyses — it is capturing a distinct long-run signal about which markets were most structurally and durably reshaped by COVID-19 as a multi-year economic event, as opposed to which markets responded most dramatically to COVID-19 as an acute financial shock.

The contrast between the Initial Shock's equity dominance and the Full Period's commodity dominance should be read as one of the project's central substantive findings: COVID's financial market impact was front-loaded into equities during the acute phase, but its durable economic footprint settled into commodity markets — particularly agricultural commodities and energy — where supply-side disruption and demand restructuring persisted long after equity markets had repriced and recovered. The full-period levels analysis is the analytical lens best positioned to capture this longer arc, and the `covid_deaths` → `agr1` pairing at its apex is the clearest quantitative expression of where COVID's causal influence ultimately resided across the pandemic's complete trajectory.

**Initial Shock Levels Analysis: Leading Relationships.** The Initial Shock levels analysis produces the highest composite scores of any phase in the project, with the top pairing — rolling, cumulative `covid_c&d` → `stk_mkt` — reaching 66.06. This figure is the project's upper bound for COVID causal influence on financial markets, and its position at the apex of the Initial Shock rankings reflects the unique conditions of February–June 2020: a period in which pandemic data was not merely one market-moving signal among many but the dominant organizing fact of global financial markets.

The transformation pattern across the top 10 rows is unambiguous and analytically meaningful. All 10 rows carry cumulative X-variables, and 8 of the 10 carry the 7-day rolling average transform of that cumulative series. This near-unanimous selection of the rolling cumulative transformation is not a statistical artifact — it reflects the specific information structure of the early pandemic. Raw daily case and death counts during the Initial Shock were highly volatile, subject to weekend reporting lags, testing capacity constraints, and jurisdictional variation in reporting protocols. The 7-day rolling average corrects for this noise by smoothing over weekly reporting cycles, while the cumulative transform captures what investors were actually tracking: the pandemic's accumulated severity and its implied trajectory, not the day-to-day fluctuation around that trajectory. ### 2.2: Percentage-Change Analysis

The percentage-change analysis addresses the most fundamental inferential challenge in time series econometrics: distinguishing genuine causal relationships from the spurious correlations that arise when two non-stationary series happen to trend together over the same window. By transforming both the COVID data series and the economic indicator series into their period-over-period percentage changes, the analysis eliminates shared trend structure by construction and forces any detected relationship to stand on its own merits as short-run predictive content.
The distinction between levels and percentage-change frameworks is not merely technical — it corresponds to two fundamentally different economic questions. The levels analysis asks whether the long-run trajectory of pandemic severity and the long-run trajectory of financial indicators moved together in a statistically disciplined way, which is the appropriate question for identifying cointegrating relationships and sustained structural influences. The percentage-change analysis asks a narrower and more demanding question: does an acceleration in COVID data today contain information about the direction of financial indicator returns in the near term, after controlling for each series' own history? A positive answer to this second question is evidence of active, short-run causal transmission — the kind of relationship that would be exploitable in real time by a market participant observing daily pandemic releases. A negative answer, even when the levels analysis finds significance, suggests that the two series share a long-run trajectory without COVID data providing incremental predictive power at the daily frequency once trend structure is removed.

The percentage-change framework imposes three properties that make it the more econometrically rigorous of the two approaches. First, stationarity is achieved by transformation rather than by assumption: both series are stationary by construction once expressed as percentage changes, eliminating the need for the differencing and unit root pre-testing that the levels analysis requires, and removing the risk that non-stationarity correction has been misapplied or that residual non-stationarity contaminates the inference. Second, the absence of shared trend structure means that Granger causality tests cannot be inflated by co-trending — any F-statistic that clears the significance threshold must reflect the COVID series' ability to predict the indicator series' short-run movements after the indicator's own lagged returns have been controlled for, which is a genuinely demanding predictive criterion. Third, the percentage-change framework is symmetric across phases: because both series are stationary by construction regardless of the underlying data-generating process, cross-phase comparisons of Granger causality strength and FEVD X-shares are not confounded by differences in the non-stationarity correction applied to each phase, making the percentage-change results the more reliable basis for the composite score comparisons that track COVID's causal attenuation across the pandemic's timeline.

The cost of these inferential advantages is a corresponding reduction in power for detecting relationships that operate at the trajectory level rather than the return level. Cointegrating relationships — in which two non-stationary series are bound by a stable long-run equilibrium — are invisible to the percentage-change framework because differencing destroys the level information that cointegration requires. Supply disruption mechanisms that build slowly over months, accommodation-driven commodity tailwinds that operate continuously rather than episodically, and structural demand shifts that reshape indicator trajectories without generating day-to-day return predictability are all examples of economically real COVID influences that the percentage-change analysis may fail to detect even when the levels analysis finds them clearly. This is not a deficiency of the percentage-change framework but a feature of its design: it is deliberately calibrated to find only the subset of COVID influences that are strong enough and fast enough to survive the stripping of trend structure.

The percentage-change analysis is therefore best understood not as a replacement for the levels analysis but as its most stringent complement. Where both frameworks agree in finding significant COVID → indicator causality, the convergence constitutes the project's strongest evidence that a genuine causal relationship exists — one that holds at both the trajectory level and the short-run return level, across both a relaxed and a demanding inferential standard. Where the frameworks diverge, the pattern of disagreement is itself analytically informative. Levels significance without percentage-change significance indicates a long-run co-trending relationship that does not extend to short-run return predictability — consistent with habituation, shared macro drivers, or slow-moving structural channels that operate below the daily frequency threshold. Percentage-change significance without levels significance indicates an episodic short-run causal channel that activates during specific sub-periods — wave resurgences, acute supply disruptions, policy inflection points — without producing the stable long-run equilibrium that cointegration requires. Both patterns appear in the project's results, and the interplay between them is one of the primary analytical tools through which the project traces the evolution of COVID's causal footprint across the pandemic's four phases.

#### Economic Context

The Initial Shock phase spans January 22 to June 7, 2020 — 137 days that constitute the most information-dense and analytically distinctive period of the four phases. The phase encompasses the full arc from the first confirmed US COVID case through the acute market crash and into the initial recovery, compressing an extraordinary sequence of pandemic developments, policy responses, and market regime shifts into a single analytical window.

The market dimension of this period is without modern precedent in its speed. The S&P 500 experienced a peak-to-trough decline of 34% in 33 days — a collapse that required 274 days to unfold during the 2008 financial crisis and 630 days during the dot-com bust. This compression is not merely a statistical curiosity: it reflects the specific nature of the COVID shock as an event that was simultaneously global in scope, immediate in economic consequence, and genuinely uncertain in duration and severity. Unlike the 2008 crisis, which unfolded through a sequence of identifiable institutional failures that markets could partially anticipate and sequence, COVID presented as a discontinuous regime change — a shock with no established pricing framework, no historical analog at the daily data frequency, and no clear endpoint. The speed of the equity repricing was a direct function of this radical uncertainty: with no precedent to anchor expectations, markets moved as fast as the pandemic data did.

The policy response was equally without precedent in its scale and speed. The Federal Reserve cut its benchmark rate by a combined 150 basis points across two emergency actions — 50 basis points on March 3 and 100 basis points on March 15 — outside its normal meeting schedule, a degree of urgency last seen in the immediate aftermath of the September 11 attacks and the 2008 Lehman collapse. It simultaneously launched unlimited quantitative easing, expanded its asset purchase programs to include corporate bonds and exchange-traded funds for the first time in its history, and established a suite of emergency lending facilities that extended its balance sheet into segments of the credit market it had never previously entered. The $2.2 trillion CARES Act, signed on March 27, 2020, represented the largest single fiscal intervention in US history at that time, providing direct payments to households, expanded unemployment insurance, and loans to businesses across the economy. These policy actions were themselves COVID data-driven: each escalation in the Federal Reserve's response and each legislative expansion of the fiscal package was triggered by or anticipated deteriorating pandemic conditions, creating the tight three-way relationship between COVID data, policy action, and market pricing that makes the Initial Shock the phase most amenable to causal identification in the project's analytical framework.

The phase's 137-day window also encompasses the beginning of the equity recovery that commenced on March 23, 2020 — the day the Federal Reserve announced unlimited asset purchases — and continued through the end of the phase in early June. This recovery creates the phase-cancellation problem documented throughout the Initial Shock analysis: the crash sub-period, in which rising COVID data coincided with falling equity prices, and the recovery sub-period, in which COVID data continued rising or plateaued while equity prices rebounded, generate opposing correlation signals that partially offset one another in aggregate window analyses. The 137-day window is therefore not a single regime but at minimum two structurally distinct sub-regimes concatenated within a single-phase boundary — a feature that qualifies but does not undermine the causal findings, and that a phase-separated analysis would be expected to recover more cleanly.

The Initial Shock's position in the project's cross-phase narrative is defined by a specific and historically bounded condition: it is the only phase in which COVID data functioned as the primary and largely exclusive driver of financial market outcomes, operating through the risk sentiment channel before markets had developed the habituation, policy anchoring, and alternative information frameworks that characterized all subsequent phases. This exclusivity is what drives the phase's composite scores to their project-maximum levels and what makes its analytical results the benchmark against which all subsequent causal attenuation is measured. It also reflects the operation of the risk sentiment channel at full intensity — the fastest, most liquid, and most immediately responsive of the three primary COVID → financial market transmission mechanisms identified across the project.

The risk sentiment channel dominated the Initial Shock precisely because the conditions for the other two channels had not yet materialized. The supply disruption channel — which would come to define the Adaptation and Recovery phases through its effects on metal and commodity markets — requires the accumulation of pandemic conditions across production networks, logistics chains, and labor markets over weeks and months before it generates statistically detectable price effects. In February and March 2020, those accumulation processes were only beginning. The relative economic damage channel — which the Full Period percentage-change analysis identifies through exchange rate leadership — requires differential pandemic trajectories across currency areas to generate the divergent growth and policy expectations that currency markets price; in the acute shock phase, the pandemic was spreading globally and approximately simultaneously across major economic areas, limiting the cross-sectional variation that the relative damage channel requires. Risk sentiment, by contrast, required none of these accumulation conditions: it activated instantaneously as pandemic severity crossed the visibility threshold in late February 2020, operated through the deepest and most liquid markets in the global financial system, and produced the project's strongest and most methodologically robust causal signal as a direct consequence.

#### Stationarity and Structural Break Diagnostics

All six-time series entering the Initial Shock analysis — the rolling cumulative COVID cases-and-deaths series and the five equity indices — are non-stationary, and the specific forms of their non-stationarity are sufficiently distinct from standard unit root processes to warrant careful documentation before the downstream causal results are interpreted.

The COVID series follows a monotonically increasing trajectory by construction: cumulative case and death counts can only rise, making the series non-stationary in the most fundamental sense — it has no mean to revert to, and its variance grows without bound as the window lengthens. Overlaid on this structural monotonicity is a major regime change in the rate of accumulation: the onset of exponential US case growth in mid-March 2020 represents a discontinuous acceleration in the series' trajectory that standard ADF and KPSS tests, designed for stationary or smoothly trending processes, are not well-equipped to characterize. The Zivot-Andrews test, which tests for a unit root while allowing for a single unknown structural break in either the level or trend of the series, and the Bai-Perron test, which identifies multiple breakpoints simultaneously, both confirm this break and locate it in the mid-March window — consistent with the epidemiological timeline of US community transmission becoming self-sustaining and the case count trajectory shifting from linear to exponential growth. This structural break is analytically important because it means the COVID series is not well-described by a single integrated process across the full 137-day window: its pre-break and post-break segments have materially different trend slopes, and a stationarity correction calibrated to the full-window properties of the series will be miscalibrated for at least one of the two sub-regimes.

The five equity index series present a different and, in some respects, more analytically challenging non-stationarity problem. Standard non-stationary financial series — equity indices in normal market conditions — are well-described as random walks with drift, where prices accumulate small daily innovations around a slowly evolving trend. The Initial Shock equity series are not of this type. They exhibit a sharp, high-magnitude downward break beginning in late February and early March 2020, followed by a recovery trend that begins around March 23 and continues through the end of the phase. The resulting shape is a V — or more precisely, an asymmetric V, since the recovery was slower and less complete than the decline — that sits outside the distributional assumptions of standard ADF and KPSS testing in two ways simultaneously. First, the downward break is large enough relative to the series' historical variance that ADF tests may incorrectly fail to reject the unit root null, mistaking the break-induced non-stationarity for the smooth drift non-stationarity the test is designed to detect. Second, the V-shape means the series is mean-reverting within the window in a mechanical sense — it returns toward its starting level by the end of the phase — which can cause KPSS tests to incorrectly indicate stationarity, since the test interprets the return toward the starting level as evidence of mean reversion rather than recognizing it as a break-and-recovery pattern superimposed on an underlying non-stationary process. The combination of ADF over-retaining the unit root null and KPSS under-retaining it produces the four-verdict inconclusive outcome — the most diagnostically challenging classification in the ADF-KPSS framework — for several of the equity series, particularly those with the sharpest crash-and-recovery profiles.

The `mathx.crct_stnry_df()` stationarity correction pipeline addresses these diagnostics systematically before any causal testing is performed. The pipeline applies the four-verdict ADF-KPSS framework to classify each series, invokes structural break detection via Zivot-Andrews and Bai-Perron where the four-verdict classification indicates trend-stationarity or inconclusiveness, selects the appropriate stationarity correction — differencing, percentage-change transformation, polynomial detrending, or break-adjusted differencing — based on the full diagnostic profile of each series rather than the output of any single test, and verifies the stationarity of the corrected series before passing it to downstream analysis. For the COVID series, this process identifies the mid-March structural break and applies a correction that accounts for the change in trend slope rather than treating the series as a smoothly integrated process throughout. For the equity indices, it navigates the V-shape diagnostic challenge by combining break detection with the four-verdict classification to distinguish between genuine non-stationarity requiring differencing and the break-contaminated inconclusive outcomes that standard tests generate for these series. The result is a set of corrected series whose stationarity properties are verified rather than assumed, and whose correction method is calibrated to the actual data-generating process of each series rather than to the idealized processes that standard tests presuppose. All Granger causality, VECM, IRF, and FEVD results reported for the Initial Shock phase are computed on these corrected series.

**Contemporaneous Correlation** 

The contemporaneous correlation between the rolling cumulative COVID C&D series and the five equity indices during the Initial Shock was expected to be strongly negative: rising case and death counts align chronologically with falling prices through the March 23 trough, while falling or stabilizing counts align with the subsequent recovery. Because all six series trend over the 137-day window, a portion of any raw correlation necessarily reflects shared trend structure rather than a genuine contemporaneous relationship — a confound the Spearman rank correlation partially addresses by operating on ranks rather than levels, but cannot eliminate entirely when both series share a common directional arc across the full window. The polynomial degree optimization provides a complementary test: if equity markets responded nonlinearly to COVID data — crashing disproportionately once case and death counts crossed a visibility threshold and producing a concave or step-function scatter profile — the optimal polynomial degree should exceed one, and the cross-validated fit should be meaningfully better than a linear baseline.

**The Correlation Results: Phase Cancellation as the Primary Explanation.** The results diverged substantially from this expectation. Rather than the anticipated strongly negative Spearman coefficients, the five indices produced weak and mixed-sign correlations with the rolling cumulative COVID series: S&P 500 (+0.35), Nasdaq (+0.04), DJIA (−0.09), NYSE (−0.10), and Russell 2000 (−0.07). Only three indices produced negative coefficients, and all five are small enough in magnitude to indicate negligible contemporaneous association at the aggregate window level. The expected strongly negative signal is absent.

**Granger Causality** 

The Granger causality analysis tests the directional asymmetry at the heart of the Initial Shock causal narrative: whether COVID data predicted equity market movements (X→Y) significantly more strongly than equity markets predicted COVID data (Y→X). A finding of asymmetric causality running from COVID to equities — and not in the reverse direction — is the minimum evidentiary standard for interpreting pandemic data as a genuine leading predictor of market behavior rather than a contemporaneous correlate of the same underlying shock. The composite score of 66.06, the project's maximum, is built substantially on the strength and consistency of this directional asymmetry across all five indices.

The directional asymmetry is confirmed across all five indices with high-confidence conclusions and no ambiguous cases in the primary direction. The unanimity of the four-test agreement at lag 1 is the strongest possible outcome the testing framework can produce: it indicates that the COVID → equity predictive relationship is robust to the specific inferential approach used to detect it, rather than being an artifact of any single test's assumptions or sensitivity. The lower F-statistics for these three indices relative to S&P 500 and Nasdaq reflect both the longer lag required for significance and the broader, more heterogeneous composition of these indices, but the four-test unanimity confirms that their lag-5 results are genuine rather than marginal.

The lag heterogeneity between the large-cap benchmarks and the broader indices is analytically meaningful rather than incidental. S&P 500 and Nasdaq — characterized by deep liquidity, high institutional participation, and concentration in sectors (technology, healthcare, consumer discretionary) whose pandemic sensitivity was most immediately legible to sophisticated investors — incorporated the COVID signal within a single trading day. DJIA, NYSE, and Russell 2000 — representing broader market compositions that include more cyclical, smaller-cap, and less liquid constituents — required five trading days to register a statistically detectable response to the same COVID information. The pandemic itself passed through at least three distinct sub-phases during Adaptation. The summer and fall of 2020 brought a second wave of infections and deaths in the United States and Europe, occurring against a backdrop of partial economic reopening and the first large-scale fiscal stimulus programs. Winter 2020–21 produced the deadliest sustained mortality surge of the entire pandemic in the US, coinciding with the Emergency Use Authorization of the Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna vaccines in December 2020 — a structural inflection point that began decoupling death-rate trajectory from market expectations about the pandemic's economic endpoint. Spring and summer 2021 brought vaccine-driven mortality decline followed by the Delta variant resurgence beginning in July 2021, which temporarily reversed that trajectory before the phase closed in November 2021. The COVID death-rate series used in this analysis — rolling cumulative deaths in percentage-change form — captures all three of these sub-regimes within a single 536-day window, which is a significant source of structural heterogeneity in the results.

The macroeconomic environment during Adaptation was simultaneously shaped by forces largely independent of the pandemic. The commodity super cycle that drove the Bloomberg Commodity Index to multi-year highs by mid-2021 was partly pandemic-related — supply chain disruption and fiscal stimulus both contributed — but also reflected structural demand shifts toward electrification and infrastructure investment that predated COVID and would have occurred regardless of the pandemic's trajectory. The Federal Reserve's commitment to near-zero interest rates and asset purchases throughout the period created a persistent monetary accommodation backdrop that supported commodity prices broadly, making it difficult to isolate COVID's marginal contribution to metal return variance from the accommodation channel's baseline effect. Global supply chain disruption, which intensified through 2021, operated through both pandemic and non-pandemic mechanisms simultaneously — semiconductor shortages, shipping congestion, and labor market tightness all intersected with ongoing COVID disruptions in ways that are analytically inseparable at the daily frequency this analysis employs.

Critically, by the start of the Adaptation phase markets had undergone a fundamental repricing of pandemic risk. The acute uncertainty that characterized the Initial Shock — when the economic consequences of COVID were genuinely unknown and market participants had no framework for pricing a novel pandemic — had been replaced by a more calibrated assessment of COVID as a persistent background condition. Investors were no longer reacting to pandemic data as if each data point carried existential implications for economic activity; they were instead incorporating it as one factor among many in an environment also shaped by monetary policy, fiscal stimulus, vaccine progress, and the structural demand shifts described above. This habituation effect is the primary reason why the Adaptation composite score of 56.60 represents a meaningful decline from the Initial Shock's 66.06, and why the analytical results across all five methods show a weaker, more episodic, and more heterogeneous COVID signal than the Initial Shock produced.

#### Why Deaths Rather Than Cases

The shift from rolling cumulative `covid_c&d` during the Initial Shock to `covid_deaths` during the Adaptation period as the dominant X-variable reflects a substantive change in the informational structure of the pandemic rather than an arbitrary modeling choice or a post-hoc rationalization of variable selection results. It is the analytical framework recovering, empirically and without prior constraint, the same informational pivot that epidemiologists, policymakers, and sophisticated market participants were making in real time as the pandemic matured.

During the Initial Shock, the combined cases-and-deaths series was the appropriate signal precisely because both components carried independent and jointly informative content. Case counts provided leading indication of mortality trajectory at a time when the infection fatality rate was genuinely uncertain and the relationship between confirmed cases and eventual deaths was an active object of scientific and market attention. Death counts provided the realized confirmation of that trajectory. The combination captured the pandemic's full epidemiological footprint — its breadth and its severity simultaneously — at a moment when both dimensions were rapidly evolving and both were being continuously repriced by financial markets operating without an established framework for valuing pandemic risk.

By June 2020, this informational equivalence had structurally broken down along several dimensions simultaneously. Testing infrastructure had expanded substantially but unevenly across jurisdictions, making confirmed case counts increasingly sensitive to testing capacity, protocol changes, and reporting definitions rather than to underlying infection prevalence. The decision to test — and therefore to confirm a case — was itself endogenous to policy choices, healthcare system capacity, and in several jurisdictions, political considerations about what numbers to report and when. A series whose level and rate of change are partly determined by administrative and political decisions rather than by the underlying epidemiological reality it purports to measure is a degraded signal for financial market analysis, regardless of how carefully it is transformed. Cumulative fatality counts are not immune to these problems — death attribution definitions varied across jurisdictions and changed over time, and reporting lags introduced their own distortions — but they are substantially harder to inflate through expanded testing capacity, substantially harder to deflate through political pressure on reporting agencies, and substantially more directly connected to the economic disruption channels that markets were pricing during the Adaptation period: labor force incapacitation, healthcare system strain, behavioral restriction, and the severity of ongoing supply chain disruption.

The vaccine rollout beginning in December 2020 introduced a further structural break that reinforced deaths as the operative signal. As vaccination rates rose through spring 2021, the previously stable relationship between case counts and mortality outcomes weakened decisively: vaccinated populations continued generating positive tests — particularly under expanded surveillance testing — but at substantially lower fatality rates than the pre-vaccine period. A rising case count in a highly vaccinated population carried fundamentally different economic implications than the same case count in an unvaccinated population, and a market participant using raw case counts as a severity proxy would systematically misread the pandemic's economic implications from late 2020 onward. The death rate, by contrast, remained a more consistent measure of pandemic severity across vaccination status strata: it continued to capture the fraction of the population experiencing severe enough illness to generate the supply chain disruption, mobility restriction, and healthcare system strain that were the Adaptation period's dominant COVID → economic transmission mechanisms.

The candidate selection analysis confirms this variable shift empirically and without analytical assumption: `covid_deaths` appears in 7 of the top 10 rows in both the Adaptation levels and percentage-change analyses, making it the dominant COVID variable by selection frequency across two independent analytical frameworks operating on different data transformations. This convergence — the levels framework selecting deaths because the deaths trajectory best captures the pandemic's accumulated economic footprint, and the percentage-change framework selecting deaths because short-run death-rate accelerations carry more reliable predictive information about commodity returns than short-run case-count accelerations — is the analytical system's confirmation that the variable shift is data-driven rather than assumption-driven. The market was watching death rates by mid-2020 because death rates had become the most reliable real-time signal of pandemic severity available; the candidate selection analysis recovers that fact from the data rather than imposing it from theory.

#### Why Metals as the Dominant Y-Variable

The emergence of metals as the most COVID-sensitive indicator category during the Adaptation period is not a statistical coincidence but the analytical expression of a fundamental shift in how the pandemic was transmitting its economic consequences. The Initial Shock operated almost exclusively through the demand-side channel: falling expectations for economic activity drove equity prices down immediately and across the board, producing a broad-based, directionally unambiguous, and institutionally liquid repricing event. By mid-2020, this channel had largely closed — equity markets had absorbed COVID as a priced background condition — and the pandemic's economic footprint was increasingly concentrated in a different and structurally slower transmission mechanism: the disruption of physical supply chains connecting the extraction, processing, and delivery of raw materials to their end markets. Metals sit at the intersection of this supply-side channel more directly than any other indicator category, which is why they emerge as the dominant Y-variable class precisely when the supply disruption mechanism becomes the primary COVID → financial market transmission pathway.

The supply-side mechanism operated through several distinct but reinforcing pathways during the Adaptation period, each connecting pandemic severity to metal prices through a different link in the production and logistics chain. Mining operations are among the most geographically concentrated and labor-intensive industrial activities in the global economy, and they proved acutely vulnerable to COVID transmission throughout the pandemic's middle phase. Major copper-producing regions in South America — Chile and Peru together accounting for approximately 40% of global copper mine supply — experienced significant workforce disruptions during the second and third waves of 2020 and into 2021, with mine operators implementing reduced-capacity protocols, quarantine requirements, and in some cases temporary shutdowns that directly constrained supply at the extraction stage. Platinum and palladium production, concentrated in South Africa and Russia respectively, faced similar disruptions. Smelting and refining operations — which require densely housed workforces operating continuous-process facilities that cannot easily be paused and restarted — were particularly vulnerable to outbreak-driven capacity reductions that translated directly into processing bottlenecks even when mine-level extraction continued. Logistics networks connecting mine outputs to end markets were simultaneously disrupted by port congestion, container shortages, and border restriction protocols that the pandemic imposed on the global shipping system, adding a further supply constraint layer that was geographically distributed and difficult to predict or hedge.

Demand conditions during the Adaptation period were simultaneously amplifying the price implications of these supply constraints rather than offsetting them. The unprecedented fiscal stimulus deployed across major economies in response to the pandemic — infrastructure packages, direct payments, and manufacturing support programs — generated a surge in structural demand for industrial metals that interacted with pandemic-constrained supply to produce the commodity super cycle conditions visible in metal prices through mid-2021. The accelerating buildout of electric vehicle battery supply chains, which had been underway before the pandemic but was turbocharged by the policy response to it, created new and price-inelastic demand streams for copper, platinum, and palladium that operated on multi-year investment cycles largely independent of short-run pandemic developments. The result was a market structure in which supply was pandemic-constrained and demand was stimulus-driven — a combination that made metal prices acutely sensitive to any incremental information about pandemic severity's effect on the supply side, without the offsetting demand-destruction dynamic that had characterized the Initial Shock period.

The COVID death rate captures this supply-side mechanism more directly and reliably than any other available pandemic data series during the Adaptation period. At the firm and operational level, death rates proxy for the severity of workforce incapacitation across the mining, smelting, and logistics operations that constitute the metal supply chain — a more stable and less administratively contaminated measure of disruption severity than case counts, which reflect testing capacity and protocol as much as actual infection prevalence. At the market level, death rates function as a regime signal: sustained elevation of the death rate indicates that the pandemic remains severe enough to maintain the supply disruption conditions that markets had learned to associate with metal price support, while declining death rates signal the potential normalization of supply chains and the removal of that support. This dual function — operational disruption proxy and regime indicator simultaneously — makes the death rate the most economically coherent X-variable for the metals analysis, and explains the specificity of the Granger causality results: silver, platinum, and copper achieving high-confidence significance at lag 1, while gold — whose price dynamics during the Adaptation period were driven primarily by monetary policy and safe-haven demand rather than supply disruption — produced no significant COVID → metal causality at any lag. The Granger framework is recovering the supply disruption channel's actual reach across the metals complex, and the channel's reach maps precisely onto the distinction between metals whose supply chains were materially disrupted by pandemic conditions and metals whose price dynamics had decoupled from those conditions by mid-2020.

#### Cross-Metal Heterogeneity

The Adaptation metals analysis produces a cross-metal pattern that is more structurally informative than a simple precious-versus-industrial divide would suggest. The results confirm that meaningful heterogeneity exists across the five metals in their COVID sensitivity during this phase, but the fault lines run through the metals complex in ways that reflect each metal's specific supply chain geography, demand driver composition, and market structure rather than the coarser precious-versus-industrial classification that might serve as a first approximation.

**Gold: Complete Decoupling.** Gold is the only metal in the group that shows no statistically detectable causal relationship with the COVID death rate in any analytical dimension — no Granger causality in either direction at any lag, no coherent directional structure in the scatter plot, no discernible temporal ordering in the lag correlation profile, and the most visually damped rolling correlation of the five. This complete decoupling is not a gradual attenuation of a relationship that was present during the Initial Shock but a categorical null: the COVID death rate carries no information relevant to gold's price dynamics that gold's own return history does not already contain. The economic explanation is that gold's dominant price drivers during the Adaptation period — real interest rates, dollar strength, and the market's assessment of the Federal Reserve's normalization timeline — were related to the pandemic's monetary policy consequences rather than to its physical severity, and those monetary consequences had been fully priced into gold by mid-2020 as the Federal Reserve's commitment to near-zero rates became unconditional. The incremental death-rate signal, which carries information about physical supply disruption and pandemic severity, was simply not the variable that gold markets were watching by the time the Adaptation phase began.

**Silver: Industrial Behavior Despite Dual Classification.** Silver's classification as a dual precious-and-industrial metal might lead to the expectation that it would occupy an intermediate position between gold's complete decoupling and copper's clear industrial responsiveness — partially COVID-sensitive through its industrial channel, partially insulated through its precious metal channel. The results do not support this intermediate characterization. Silver behaves as a fully industrial metal for the purposes of this analysis: it achieves Granger causality from COVID deaths at lag 1 with identical test statistics to copper (F=5.28, p=0.02), produces a U-shaped scatter profile indistinguishable from copper's, and shows rolling correlation dynamics that track copper's oscillation pattern more closely than gold's. The precious metal channel — through which silver might be expected to behave like gold, decoupled from physical supply disruption signals — appears to have been subordinate to the industrial channel during the Adaptation period, consistent with silver's industrial demand share having grown substantially through the solar panel and electronics supply chains that the pandemic's supply disruption dynamics directly affected. Silver's behavior in this analysis is therefore not anomalous but economically coherent: when supply disruption is the dominant COVID transmission mechanism, the industrial dimension of a dual-use metal determines its COVID sensitivity, and silver's industrial footprint was large enough during this phase to pull its analytical behavior entirely into the industrial metal category.

**Platinum: The Purest Supply Disruption Signal.** Platinum produces the most coherent and multi-method-consistent COVID signal of any metal in the Adaptation analysis — the only positive directional scatter profile, Granger causality at lag 1 (F=4.23, p=0.04) with the highest FEVD X-share of the group (4.2%), a positive cumulative IRF response with the fastest peak lag (lag 1, magnitude +6.33), and the most pronounced rolling correlation elevation during the Delta wave resurgence. This consistency across five analytical methods is the analytical signature of a metal whose COVID sensitivity operates through a single dominant and well-defined channel — South African mine and processing disruption — rather than through the competing channels that dilute or reverse the signal for other metals. Platinum's geographic production concentration makes it the metal most directly and reliably connected to pandemic severity in a specific producing region, and the results reflect that concentration with a degree of cross-method consistency that no other metal achieves during the Adaptation phase.

**Palladium: Demand-Side Dominance Overriding Supply Disruption.** Palladium's analytical profile is the most paradoxical in the group: an industrial metal with highly concentrated production in Russia and South Africa — both pandemic-affected producing regions — that nonetheless produces no significant COVID → metal Granger causality and generates a negative cumulative IRF response, indicating that COVID shocks were associated with falling rather than rising palladium returns. The resolution of this paradox lies in palladium's exceptional demand concentration: approximately 85% of palladium demand comes from automotive catalytic converters, making it uniquely dependent on vehicle production volumes in a way that no other metal in the group replicates. During the Adaptation period, the dominant disruption to vehicle production was not pandemic-driven labor shortages but semiconductor chip shortages — a supply chain crisis that was pandemic-adjacent in its origins but had become structurally autonomous by 2021, driven by chip fabrication capacity constraints that the COVID death rate does not proxy. When the COVID death rate accelerated during the Delta wave, palladium markets interpreted the signal not as supply constraint for the metal but as demand destruction for the vehicles that consume it — a channel interpretation that runs opposite to the supply disruption channel operating for copper and platinum, and that the IRF's negative sign captures precisely. Palladium's COVID insensitivity in the Granger framework and its negative IRF response are therefore not evidence of a measurement failure but of a genuine economic structure in which the demand destruction channel dominated the supply disruption channel for this specific metal during this specific phase.

**Copper: The Canonical Industrial Channel.** Copper occupies the analytical position most directly consistent with the supply disruption narrative that motivates the Adaptation metals analysis. Its Granger result at lag 1 (F=5.28, p=0.02), U-shaped scatter profile, positive cumulative IRF (+23.06), and rolling correlation elevation during the Delta wave all point to the same mechanism: pandemic death-rate acceleration constraining supply from the South American producing regions that dominate global copper output, generating upward price pressure that copper markets price within one to five trading days. Copper's status as the most widely used industrial metal — a barometer of global economic activity whose supply disruption implications are immediately visible to institutional participants across manufacturing, construction, and infrastructure sectors — makes it the most direct and liquid conduit for the supply disruption channel to reach metal prices during the Adaptation period. Its near-identical results to silver across multiple analytical dimensions confirm that the supply disruption channel, when active, operates with comparable strength across both metals — but copper's purely industrial demand structure means its COVID sensitivity is unambiguous in direction and mechanism in a way that silver's dual-use nature complicates.

**The Heterogeneity Pattern as a Structural Finding.** Read as a whole, the cross-metal heterogeneity of the Adaptation results reveals a causal structure organized not by the precious-versus-industrial classification but by two more specific distinctions: whether a metal's supply chain is geographically concentrated in pandemic-affected producing regions (determining the strength of the supply disruption channel), and whether a metal's demand structure is dominated by a single sector with its own idiosyncratic disruption dynamics (determining whether demand-side noise overwhelms the supply disruption signal). Platinum and copper satisfy the first condition most clearly and produce the clearest COVID causal signals. Silver satisfies it partially but sufficiently, through its industrial demand footprint, to produce equivalent Granger results. Palladium satisfies the supply concentration condition but is overridden by its automotive demand concentration, producing a null Granger result and a negative IRF. Gold satisfies neither condition in a way relevant to the COVID death rate signal, producing complete decoupling. This five-metal pattern is the Adaptation phase's most precise characterization of which properties of a commodity market determine its COVID sensitivity when the pandemic's dominant transmission mechanism is physical supply disruption rather than broad-based risk sentiment.

#### Percentage-Change Framework

The Adaptation phase metals analysis operates entirely in percentage-change form for both series: the COVID death rate is expressed as the period-over-period percentage change in the rolling cumulative deaths count, and each metal's return series is expressed as its daily percentage price change. This transformation choice is not a default setting but a substantive analytical decision whose implications run through every result in the section and whose rationale connects directly to the structural differences between the Adaptation period and the Initial Shock.

During the Initial Shock, the levels framework was appropriate because both the COVID series and the equity indices were non-stationary and trending — the pandemic's cumulative toll was rising monotonically, equity prices were falling and then recovering along a structured arc, and the cointegrating relationships between them captured a genuine long-run equilibrium dynamic in which shared trend structure was economically meaningful rather than spurious. The Adaptation period presents a fundamentally different analytical environment. A 536-day window spanning multiple pandemic waves, a vaccine rollout, a commodity super cycle, and the onset of Federal Reserve tightening contains too much structural heterogeneity for a shared trend between the COVID series and metal prices to carry a stable, interpretable long-run meaning. Any correlation detected at the levels frequency across this window risks reflecting the coincidental co-movement of two independently trending series — pandemic mortality declining as metals prices rose during the super cycle, or vice versa — rather than a genuine causal relationship operating through the supply disruption mechanism the analysis is designed to identify. The percentage-change transformation addresses this directly: by stripping both series down to their period-over-period movements, it eliminates shared trend structure by construction and forces any detected relationship to stand on its own as short-run predictive content.

The specific question the percentage-change framework asks is correspondingly precise: does an acceleration in the COVID death rate — a day on which the percentage change in cumulative deaths is larger than usual — contain predictive information about the direction of metal returns in the near term, after controlling for each metal's own return history? This is a more demanding evidentiary standard than the levels analysis in three distinct ways. It requires the COVID signal to operate at the daily return frequency rather than at the trajectory level, meaning relationships that accumulate slowly over weeks or months may not be detectable even if they are economically real. It requires the signal to survive the removal of all shared trend structure, eliminating the co-trending inflation that can make spurious level relationships appear significant. And it requires the COVID series to add predictive power beyond what each metal's own lagged returns already explain — the Granger causality criterion in its strictest form — rather than merely being correlated with metal returns in a bivariate sense.

The cost of this demanding standard is a corresponding reduction in signal-to-noise ratio. The percentage change in a smoothed cumulative death count is, by construction, a small number fluctuating around a slowly declining trend as cumulative totals grow — and daily metal returns are among the noisiest financial series available, driven at the daily frequency by a dense mix of macroeconomic data releases, currency movements, positioning flows, and commodity-specific news that the COVID death rate can only partially explain even when the supply disruption channel is genuinely active. The scatter plots, lag correlations, and rolling correlations throughout this section all reflect this elevated noise environment: data clouds are diffuse, lag profiles are flat, and rolling correlations oscillate rapidly across the full ±1 range. These characteristics are not evidence of a miscalibrated model or an absent relationship — they are the expected statistical profile of a genuine but episodic causal signal operating within a high-noise environment across a structurally heterogeneous 536-day window. The Granger causality results, which find high-confidence significance for silver, platinum, and copper at lag 1 despite this noise environment, are correspondingly more meaningful for having cleared a bar that the signal-to-noise ratio of the percentage-change framework makes genuinely difficult to clear.

#### Key Analytical Findings

**Contemporaneous Correlation**

The scatter plots provide the first and most visually direct assessment of the COVID death rate's relationship with metal returns during the Adaptation period, and they reveal a cross-metal structural heterogeneity that maps onto the economic distinctions between the five metals more precisely than a uniform signal would permit. Rather than a single relationship type diluted or strengthened across the group, the five scatter profiles divide into three structurally distinct patterns — each with a different economic interpretation and each consistent with a different transmission channel dominating for that metal's specific market structure.

**Gold: No Contemporaneous Signal.** The gold scatter plot is the clearest null result of the five. The data cloud is diffuse and approximately circular, with no visually detectable directional structure in either the positive or negative quadrant. The degree-2 polynomial fit — with a quadratic coefficient of 2.65e-02 and a shallow, nearly flat fitted curve — reflects the optimizer's inability to find meaningful structure rather than the presence of a genuine quadratic relationship: the fit is essentially horizontal across the bulk of the data range, with modest curvature at the extremes driven by a small number of outlier observations rather than by the cloud's central tendency. The economic interpretation is straightforward and consistent with the Granger causality results that follow: gold's price dynamics during the Adaptation period were driven primarily by real interest rate movements, dollar strength, and monetary policy expectations rather than by the supply disruption mechanism that connects pandemic mortality to metal production capacity. Gold mining was disrupted during the Adaptation period, but gold's role as a monetary metal means its price is determined at the margin by financial flows rather than by physical supply-demand balances, insulating it from the supply disruption signal that the COVID death rate carries.

**Silver: Volatility Coupling With Dual-Use Sensitivity.** Silver produces a U-shaped scatter profile with a degree-2 polynomial fit whose upward curvature is approximately symmetric around zero on the x-axis: extreme death-rate accelerations in either the positive or negative direction are associated with elevated silver returns, while moderate death-rate changes cluster near zero returns with high variance and no directional structure. This U-shape is the scatter signature of volatility coupling rather than directional causation — large COVID death-rate movements, in either direction, signal a high-volatility macro regime in which silver returns are also elevated in absolute magnitude, but the direction of that return is not systematically connected to the sign of the death-rate change. Silver's dual nature as both a precious metal — subject to the same safe-haven and monetary policy dynamics as gold — and an industrial metal — exposed to the same supply disruption and demand channels as copper and platinum — produces this symmetric volatility response: positive death-rate shocks trigger industrial supply disruption support while simultaneously triggering safe-haven demand, and negative death-rate shocks trigger recovery optimism on the industrial side while reducing safe-haven demand, with the two channels partially offsetting in the directional dimension but reinforcing in the volatility dimension. The Granger causality results subsequently show that silver does carry directional predictive information from the COVID death rate at lag 1 — a finding not contradicted by the U-shaped scatter, which captures only the contemporaneous zero-lag relationship and cannot detect the lagged predictive structure that Granger tests identify.

**Platinum: The Supply Disruption Signal Made Visible.** Platinum is the only metal among the five whose scatter plot shows a visually coherent directional structure. The data cloud slopes upward from left to right — higher death-rate acceleration associated with higher platinum returns across the bulk of the observable range — and the degree-5 polynomial fit tracks this upward tendency reasonably well through the cloud's central mass. The positive directionality is consistent with the supply disruption channel operating more directly and dominantly for platinum than for any other metal in the group: platinum production is highly concentrated in South Africa, where pandemic conditions during the Adaptation period were severe and persistently disruptive to the deep-level mining operations that platinum extraction requires. A positive death-rate acceleration was therefore more reliably associated with platinum supply constraint — and hence upward price pressure — than for metals whose production is more geographically diversified or whose supply disruption exposure operates through more diffuse pathways. The degree-5 polynomial reflects genuine complexity in the scatter structure rather than overfitting: the relationship is not linear but neither is it symmetric, with the positive association more consistent at moderate death-rate accelerations and more variable at the tails where outlier observations introduce noise. This is the expected profile of a supply disruption channel that is real but episodic — active when pandemic severity reaches a threshold sufficient to trigger operational disruption, attenuated when death-rate changes are too small to cross that threshold.

**Palladium: Compositional Decoupling.** Palladium shows the shallowest and most structurally simple scatter of the five — a degree-1 linear fit with a coefficient of 7.69e-03 and a near-horizontal fitted line sitting amid a data cloud whose vertical spread at any given x-value dwarfs the fitted relationship's slope across the entire observable x-range. The near-zero slope and enormous conditional variance indicate that the COVID death rate carries essentially no contemporaneous information about palladium returns at the zero-lag frequency. Palladium's price movements during the Adaptation period were determined more by the automotive production cycle — itself disrupted by semiconductor shortages that were pandemic-adjacent but not pandemic-caused in the direct supply disruption sense — than by the mining and logistics disruption that the COVID death rate most directly proxies. The scatter plot's flatness is therefore not evidence that supply disruption was entirely absent for palladium but that it was overwhelmed by an idiosyncratic demand-side driver that the COVID death rate does not capture and that dominated palladium's return variance during this specific phase.

**Copper: Industrial Demand Volatility Coupling.** Copper produces a scatter profile that is structurally near-identical to silver's — a U-shaped cloud with a degree-2 polynomial fit showing symmetric upward curvature around zero — and the similarity is economically meaningful rather than coincidental. Copper is the most purely industrial of the five metals, with its demand driven overwhelmingly by construction, infrastructure, and manufacturing activity rather than by monetary or safe-haven dynamics. During the Adaptation period, copper's price was simultaneously supported by fiscal stimulus-driven infrastructure demand and constrained by pandemic disruption to Chilean and Peruvian mining operations — the world's two largest copper-producing countries — creating a market structure in which large death-rate movements in either direction generated elevated return volatility without a consistent directional association. A large positive death-rate shock signaled renewed supply disruption in producing regions, supporting price; a large negative death-rate shock signaled demand recovery acceleration as economies reopened, also supporting price through the demand channel. Moderate death-rate changes, falling below the threshold that triggers either mechanism reliably, produced no systematic return direction — generating the flat, high-variance central region of the U-shaped cloud. As with silver, the U-shape captures volatility coupling at the contemporaneous frequency; the Granger causality results at lag 1 — identical F-statistic and p-value to silver (F=5.28, p=0.02) — confirm that directional predictive content exists in the lagged rather than the contemporaneous relationship, consistent with supply disruption effects propagating through copper's production and logistics chain over a one-to-five-day horizon before fully registering in spot prices.

**Granger Causality** 

The Granger causality results reveal a causal structure that is partial, asymmetric, and metal-specific — meaningfully weaker than the Initial Shock's five-for-five directional sweep but far from absent, and structured in a way that maps precisely onto the economic distinctions between the five metals identified in the scatter plot analysis. The overall picture is one of selective causal transmission: the COVID death rate retains genuine short-run predictive power for the metals whose supply chains were most directly and operationally exposed to pandemic disruption, while producing no detectable signal for the metals whose price dynamics had decoupled from that disruption channel by the Adaptation period.

**The COVID → Metal Direction: Three Confirmations, Two Nulls.** Silver and copper achieve the clearest and most unambiguous causal results of any metal in the Adaptation analysis. Both produce high-confidence significance at lag 1 with identical test statistics — F=5.28, p=0.02 — with all four test statistics in full agreement in each case. The identical F-statistics and p-values across two structurally different metals are not a coincidence of rounding but reflect the same underlying causal mechanism operating through the same lag structure: the COVID death rate acceleration contains one-day-ahead predictive information about both silver and copper returns that survives the Granger framework's controls for each metal's own return history. For copper, this result is consistent with the supply disruption channel operating through South American mine and processing disruption — death-rate accelerations in major producing regions translate into next-day supply constraint expectations that copper spot markets price within a single trading session. For silver, it reflects the metal's industrial demand sensitivity operating alongside its supply disruption exposure, with the Granger framework recovering the directional predictive content that the contemporaneous scatter's U-shaped volatility coupling obscured at the zero-lag frequency.

Platinum achieves significance at lag 1 with slightly weaker but fully confirmed statistics — F=4.23, p=0.04, all four tests in agreement, high-confidence conclusion — consistent with the supply disruption channel operating through South African deep-level mining, where the translation from pandemic severity to operational disruption is direct but subject to a slightly longer operational response time than copper's more globally distributed production network. The lag-1 result for platinum, combined with its positive directional scatter profile and its positive IRF response documented in the VAR/VECM analysis, produces the most coherent and multi-method-consistent causal picture of any metal in the Adaptation analysis: the COVID death rate acceleration predicts higher platinum returns one day ahead, the supply disruption channel provides the mechanism, and the positive IRF confirms that the cumulative effect of COVID shocks on platinum is directionally consistent with that mechanism across a 30-period horizon.

Gold produces no significant Granger causality result across any of the eight lags tested, with uniformly high p-values throughout — the minimum F-statistic of 0.22 at lag 1 is not in proximity to conventional significance thresholds at any lag — and a high-confidence non-causality conclusion across the board. This is the cleanest null result in the Adaptation metals analysis and the most economically interpretable: gold's price dynamics during this period were governed by real interest rate movements, dollar strength, and the market's evolving expectations about the pace and timing of Federal Reserve normalization — the monetary accommodation channel — rather than by the supply disruption mechanism that the COVID death rate proxies. Gold mining was disrupted during the Adaptation period, but gold's monetary metal status means that financial flows, not physical supply-demand balances, determine its price at the margin, and the COVID death rate carries no systematic short-run information about those financial flows after controlling for gold's own return history.

Palladium produces no significant COVID → metal causality across any of the eight lags tested, with the best F-statistic reaching only 1.74 at lag 5 — below conventional significance thresholds and without corroboration from the chi², lrtest, or params-F statistics. This null result is consistent with palladium's Adaptation period price dynamics being dominated by automotive sector demand and semiconductor-driven vehicle production disruptions — a demand-side idiosyncratic driver that the COVID death rate does not capture — rather than by the supply disruption channel that drives the silver, platinum, and copper results. The absence of a COVID → palladium causal signal is not evidence that palladium was unaffected by the pandemic but that the specific pandemic severity measure the analysis employs — the death rate — does not carry the information most relevant to palladium's dominant price driver during this phase.

**The Reverse Direction: One Anomalous Finding.** The metal → COVID direction produces a single significant result that warrants careful interpretation: palladium Granger-causes the COVID death rate at lag 6 (F=2.18, p=0.04), with all four test statistics in agreement and a high-confidence conclusion. The economic interpretation of this result is almost certainly not that palladium price movements caused pandemic mortality outcomes — no plausible mechanism connects daily palladium returns to COVID fatality rates six days later. The most defensible interpretation is that palladium returns and the COVID death rate share a common driver operating at a six-day lag frequency — most plausibly the same supply chain disruption dynamics that affected both palladium's industrial supply networks and the logistical, healthcare, and reporting infrastructure through which COVID mortality data is generated and released. A common driver that affects both series with slightly different transmission speeds would produce exactly this pattern in the Granger framework: apparent reverse causality that is actually the statistical signature of a confounding variable operating differentially across the two series rather than a genuine causal link running from prices to mortality. No other metal produces a significant reverse causality result at any lag, and the DJIA, NYSE, and Russell 2000 in the Initial Shock analysis demonstrated that isolated partial significance driven by a single test statistic without four-way agreement is unreliable — here, all four statistics agree, but the economic implausibility of the mechanism is sufficiently strong to warrant treating the result as a confounding artifact pending further identification.

**The Overall Causal Picture.** The summary table confirms the Adaptation Granger causality structure with precision: three of five metals — silver, platinum, copper — show high-confidence COVID → metal causality; two — gold and palladium — do not; and only the economically anomalous palladium reverse result disrupts what is otherwise a clean directional asymmetry from pandemic severity to metal returns. This three-for-five causal confirmation rate, against the Initial Shock's five-for-five sweep, is the Granger framework's quantitative expression of COVID's causal attenuation between the two phases — not a collapse of the causal relationship but a narrowing of its reach to the specific metals whose supply chains remained operationally connected to pandemic severity conditions throughout the Adaptation period's 536-day window.

**VAR/VECM Dynamics**

All five metal pairs were modeled using VECM(lag=7, rank=1), with BIC selecting lag 8 — VECM lag 7 — uniformly across the group. This uniformity is the first structurally informative result of the VAR/VECM analysis: the same lag structure fitting all five metals, despite their substantially different market structures, supply chain geographies, and demand driver compositions, suggests that the COVID death rate signal operates through a common information processing horizon of approximately one to two trading weeks across the metals complex as a whole. Whatever the specific channel — supply disruption for copper and platinum, volatility coupling for silver, decoupled automotive dynamics for palladium — the temporal scale at which the pandemic signal works through metal return dynamics is consistent across the group. All five metal return series are stationary while the COVID deaths percentage-change series is non-stationary, and cointegration rank 1 is confirmed by both trace and max-eigenvalue tests in every case, justifying the VECM specification throughout and establishing that each metal maintains a stable long-run equilibrium relationship with the pandemic death rate series despite operating through structurally different short-run channels.

**FEVD X-Shares: The Attenuation Quantified.** The Forecast Error Variance Decomposition results are the Adaptation phase's most decisive quantitative statement about the scale of COVID's causal attenuation relative to the Initial Shock, and they warrant careful reading. The FEVD X-shares across all five metals are uniformly and strikingly low: gold (2.1%), silver (3.2%), copper (3.2%), palladium (1.9%), and platinum (4.2%). Every metal is classified as minimal influence, and in every case the metal's own return shocks account for 96–98% of its forecast error variance — leaving COVID death-rate shocks to explain the residual 2–4%. The contrast with the Initial Shock's S&P 500 FEVD X-share of 48.7% is the sharpest single numerical comparison the project produces: a relationship that explained nearly half of the dominant equity index's forecast variance during the acute shock phase explains at most 4% of any metal's forecast variance during the Adaptation period, even for the metal — platinum — with the strongest Granger causality result and the most coherent supply disruption narrative.

This contrast does not mean the Granger causality results for silver, platinum, and copper are wrong or overstated. The two metrics are measuring different things at different temporal scales. Granger causality at lag 1 detects whether COVID death-rate acceleration contains any statistically significant predictive information about next-period metal returns — a binary question about the presence of a signal. The FEVD X-share measures what fraction of total return variance that signal explains across a 31-period horizon — a question about the signal's magnitude relative to all other return drivers simultaneously. A signal can be statistically real and economically present while explaining only 3–4% of variance if it competes with a dense set of other return drivers — commodity-specific supply events, currency movements, macroeconomic data releases, positioning flows — each of which contributes independently to the metal's daily return variance. During the Adaptation period, that competitive environment was exceptionally dense: the commodity super cycle, Federal Reserve policy evolution, China's zero-COVID lockdown dynamics, and the Russia-Ukraine conflict's preliminary rumblings were all operating simultaneously as metal return drivers, each with its own variance contribution that the FEVD correctly attributes to the metal's own dynamics rather than to COVID shocks. The FEVD X-shares are therefore not contradicting the Granger results — they are contextualizing them: the COVID signal was statistically real but economically minor, present in the return-generating process but not dominant within it.

**IRF Sign Heterogeneity: Channels Identified Through Direction.** The impulse response functions introduce a cross-metal structural finding that the FEVD's uniform smallness cannot reveal: significant heterogeneity in the sign of the cumulative COVID shock response, which maps onto the transmission channel distinctions identified throughout the analysis. Gold, silver, copper, and platinum all produce positive cumulative x→y responses over the 30-period horizon — a one-standard-deviation shock to the COVID death rate acceleration is associated with net positive returns for these four metals. The magnitudes vary meaningfully across the group: platinum (+22.85) and the silver-copper pair (+23.06) show substantially larger cumulative positive responses than gold (+8.96), consistent with the supply disruption channel adding to the monetary accommodation channel for the industrial metals while gold responds only to the accommodation component. Peak lag timing also differentiates the metals: platinum responds fastest with a peak at lag 1 (magnitude +6.33), consistent with its concentrated South African production making supply constraint implications immediate and highly visible to market participants, while gold, silver, and copper all peak at lag 5, suggesting a slightly longer price discovery process for these metals' supply disruption implications.

Palladium is the sole exception to the positive cumulative IRF pattern, producing a negative cumulative response of −6.66 peaking at lag 4 (magnitude −4.35). This negative sign is the IRF's confirmation of the risk-off and demand destruction channel dominating for palladium: rising COVID deaths, for this metal, are associated with falling returns over the 30-period horizon — consistent with death-rate acceleration being interpreted by palladium markets as demand destruction for the automotive sector that consumes 85% of palladium output, rather than as supply constraint for the South African and Russian mining operations that produce it. The automotive demand interpretation dominates the supply disruption interpretation for palladium in a way that it does not for any other metal in the group, and the IRF's negative sign is the cleanest evidence of this channel distinction that the VAR/VECM framework produces.

**Stability and Mean Reversion.** All five models return infinite error correction half-lives, meaning that while cointegrating relationships between the COVID death rate and each metal are confirmed, none of the five systems generates mean reversion within the 30-period IRF horizon. The cointegrating equilibria exist but do not self-correct at the pace that a stable long-run relationship would imply — consistent with the Adaptation period's structural heterogeneity making the equilibrium itself a moving target rather than a fixed attractor. Platinum and palladium are classified as is_stable = True in the summary table, indicating better-conditioned VECM dynamics and more reliable IRF profiles for these two metals. Gold, silver, and copper are classified as is_stable = False, with mean reversion not confirmed — a diagnostic flag that warrants interpretive caution for those three IRF profiles specifically, as the positive cumulative responses for these metals may reflect diverging rather than settling dynamics, making the 30-period cumulative magnitudes upper-bound estimates rather than converged equilibrium effects.

**The Adaptation VAR/VECM Finding in Context.** The VAR/VECM results for the Adaptation phase tell a coherent and internally consistent story across all five metals: COVID death-rate shocks produced statistically detectable impulse responses with directional patterns consistent with the supply disruption and monetary accommodation channels for four of five metals, and with the demand destruction channel for one. But the magnitude of this influence — FEVD X-shares uniformly below 5%, sitting 44 percentage points below the Initial Shock's S&P 500 benchmark — establishes unambiguously that the pandemic had been reduced from a dominant market-moving force to a statistically present but economically minor contributing factor in metal return dynamics. Each metal's own momentum, sector-specific supply and demand conditions, and the dense macroeconomic environment of the 2020–21 commodity super cycle accounted for the overwhelming majority of return variance that the COVID signal could not explain. The Adaptation VAR/VECM results are the project's most precise quantitative statement of what causal attenuation looks like when measured at the variance decomposition level rather than at the significance threshold level — and the answer is a reduction from near-dominance to marginal contribution, achieved over approximately six months of pandemic normalization.

**Rolling Correlation**

The rolling correlation results across all five metals share a single dominant structural feature: rapid, persistent sign-switching throughout the entire 536-day Adaptation window, with no metal sustaining a directional signal for more than a few weeks at a time and all five oscillating between extreme positive and negative values with comparable frequency and amplitude. This behavior is structurally inconsistent with any single causal channel dominating continuously: a persistent supply disruption channel would produce sustained negative rolling correlations during periods of rising deaths and positive correlations during declining deaths, following the death rate's wave structure; a persistent monetary accommodation channel would produce sustained positive correlations as rising deaths reinforced accommodation expectations; a persistent risk-off channel would produce sustained negative correlations as rising deaths triggered commodity demand destruction. None of these patterns is visible. What the rolling correlation charts show instead is a causal relationship that activates episodically, reverses sign within short windows, and leaves no persistent directional footprint at the rolling window frequency — consistent with the FEVD X-shares' finding that COVID shocks explain only 2–4% of each metal's return variance, a magnitude too small to generate the persistent rolling correlation signal that a dominant channel would produce.

Gold is the most visually damped of the five — its rolling correlation oscillates with somewhat lower amplitude and spends more time in the near-zero range than the industrial metals — consistent with its Granger non-result and with gold's price dynamics being governed by financial flows rather than supply disruption dynamics during this period. Platinum and palladium show more idiosyncratic profiles relative to the silver-copper pair, with platinum's oscillations more frequently touching the positive extreme during periods of death-rate elevation — consistent with its supply disruption exposure — and palladium's profile showing more frequent negative excursions during the same periods, consistent with the demand destruction channel that its negative IRF response identified.

**The Delta Wave as a Natural Experiment.** The most analytically significant feature of the rolling correlation charts is a visible elevation of positive rolling correlation values across multiple metals during the July–September 2021 period — the window corresponding to the Delta variant resurgence, which produced the sharpest acceleration in the COVID death rate since the winter 2020–21 surge after a prolonged period of declining mortality through spring 2021. This elevation is visible in the individual charts for copper and platinum most clearly, and to a lesser degree for silver, with gold and palladium showing less pronounced responses during the same window. The timing — a temporary strengthening of positive COVID → metal rolling correlation precisely during a period when the death rate accelerated sharply after a quiet inter-wave interval — is consistent with the supply disruption channel reactivating episodically in response to a mortality shock large enough to cross the operational disruption threshold, after having been dormant or attenuated during the preceding low-mortality months. It provides the clearest within-sample evidence that the COVID → metals causal channel did not disappear during the Adaptation period but rather entered a state of conditional activation — present when pandemic severity crossed a threshold sufficient to generate genuine supply chain disruption, absent when severity was low enough that markets had normalized their assessment of pandemic-related supply risk.

The Delta wave reactivation is more pronounced for copper and platinum than for silver and gold, a cross-metal ordering that is consistent with the supply disruption channel being the dominant mechanism when the channel activates: copper and platinum, whose supply chains are most directly and geographically concentrated in pandemic-affected producing regions, show the strongest positive rolling correlation response to death-rate acceleration, while gold — whose price is largely insensitive to physical supply disruption — shows the weakest. This ordering mirrors the Granger causality results' cross-metal pattern and reinforces the interpretation that the rolling correlation's episodic activations during wave events are genuine expressions of the supply disruption channel operating at the threshold level rather than statistical noise or mechanical artifacts of the rolling window methodology.

**Rolling Correlation and the Composite Score.** The rolling correlation's sign-switching behavior across all five metals contributes to the Adaptation composite score of 56.60 in a way that is structurally different from the Initial Shock's rolling correlation contribution. During the Initial Shock, the rolling correlation component was itself a source of analytical complexity — its sign-switching pattern was unexpected and qualified the composite score's interpretation. During the Adaptation period, the sign-switching pattern is the expected and theoretically coherent result: a causal relationship that is episodic, channel-heterogeneous, and operating in a high-noise environment should produce episodic rolling correlations with near-zero means and high standard deviations, which is precisely what the five metal charts show. The rolling correlation results are therefore not undermining the Adaptation causal narrative but confirming its specific character — a relationship that is real when activated by sufficient pandemic severity, invisible when severity falls below the activation threshold, and structurally incapable of sustaining the directional persistence that a dominant single-channel causal relationship would produce.

**Lag Correlation** 

The lag correlation results across all five metals are the most uniform and least differentiated of any analytical component in the Adaptation metals analysis — and that uniformity is itself the finding. Every metal produces a peak r-value well below the ±0.30 significance threshold, with profiles so flat and featureless across the full ±25-day lag range that the peak designations are better understood as the highest points of a noise floor than as evidence of any genuine leading or lagging structure. Gold peaks at lag −15 (r=+0.079), silver at lag +5 (r=+0.124), platinum at lag +10 (r=+0.118), palladium at lag +19 (r=+0.094), and copper at lag +5 (r=+0.124). These values are not merely below the significance threshold — they are so far below it, and so evenly distributed across the lag range without any coherent clustering, that no temporal ordering between the COVID death rate and metal returns is detectable at the aggregate window frequency regardless of which direction or lag distance is examined.

**The Profile Shape: A Noise Floor Across the Full Lag Range.** The defining characteristic of all five lag correlation profiles is not the location of the peak but the flatness of the entire profile around it. Each metal's lag correlation curve sits in a narrow band just above zero — typically between r=0.00 and r=+0.13 — across the complete ±25-day range, with no meaningful asymmetry between the positive-lag half (COVID leads metal) and the negative-lag half (metal leads COVID), and no coherent rise toward any peak that would suggest a genuine temporal relationship emerging at a specific lag distance. For gold, the profile is so flat that the designation of lag −15 as the peak reflects a marginal elevation of a fraction of an r-unit above adjacent lags rather than any structurally distinguishable feature. For palladium, the peak at lag +19 sits at the far edge of the lag range where sample size reduction and edge effects make peak designations least reliable. For silver and copper — the two metals with the strongest Granger causality results — the peak at lag +5 is the most plausible candidate for a genuine leading structure, but at r=+0.124 it falls so far short of the significance threshold that it cannot be distinguished from the chance variation in a flat profile with confidence.

The composite heatmap (Figure 1.1.8.2) makes the cross-metal uniformity of this flatness visible in a single image. The heatmap is dominated by uniform mid-green across all five metals and all lag values from −24 to +24 — the color signature of near-zero correlations that do not differentiate meaningfully across the lag space. There is no coherent band of elevated correlation in the positive-lag region that would indicate COVID systematically leading metal returns, no band in the negative-lag region that would indicate metals systematically leading COVID, and no metal row that stands out from the others as carrying more temporal structure than its neighbors. The heatmap is as close to a null result as the lag correlation framework can produce — a finding that COVID death-rate changes and metal return changes share no systematic temporal ordering at any lag from one day to twenty-four days in either direction, across the full 536-day Adaptation window.

**Reconciling Lag Correlation Nulls with Granger Causality Significance.** The tension between the lag correlation's uniform nulls and the Granger causality results — which find high-confidence COVID → metal significance at lag 1 for silver, platinum, and copper — is resolvable through two complementary explanations that operate simultaneously. The first is methodological: the lag correlation framework computes bivariate cross-correlations on the raw series without controlling for autocorrelation, meaning that each metal's own strong return autocorrelation dominates its cross-correlation profile with the COVID series, suppressing the COVID signal below the threshold of bivariate detectability even when that signal is genuine and recoverable by a framework — like Granger causality — that explicitly controls for own-series dynamics. The second is temporal: the Granger framework operates on the full 536-day window simultaneously and detects the average predictive content of the COVID signal across the entire period, but the lag correlation aggregates across the same window without the ability to distinguish between sub-periods when the COVID → metal relationship was active and sub-periods when it was dormant. If the causal relationship was genuinely active only during the Delta wave resurgence of July–September 2021 and the winter 2020–21 mortality surge — the two sub-periods where the rolling correlation analysis identified elevated COVID → metal signal — then the lag correlation's aggregate profile would average a strong positive-lag structure during those sub-periods with a flat or null structure during the intervening months, producing precisely the near-zero aggregate profile observed across all five metals.

**The Habituation Channel Confirmed at the Aggregate Frequency.** The lag correlation results are the most direct evidence available in this analytical framework for the habituation channel's operation during the Adaptation period. Habituation — the market's progressive discounting of incremental pandemic data as COVID became a priced background condition — predicts exactly this result: at the aggregate window level, daily death-rate changes carry no systematic leading information about metal return direction because markets had learned to incorporate pandemic risk as a persistent structural condition rather than responding to each incremental data point as new causal information. The lag correlation, which operates at the aggregate frequency without sub-period discrimination, is the analytical lens most sensitive to this aggregate-level null — and it finds it clearly and consistently across all five metals. The rolling correlation's episodic activations during wave events, and the Granger causality's detection of average lag-1 predictive content, capture the residual COVID signal that survived habituation during periods of sufficient pandemic severity; the lag correlation captures the habituation itself, visible as the absence of any temporal structure in the aggregate cross-correlation between pandemic mortality and metal returns across the full 536-day Adaptation window.

#### Composite Score and Phase Interpretation

The Adaptation composite score of 56.60 — a decline of 9.46 points from the Initial Shock's 66.06 — is the project's first quantitative measurement of COVID's causal attenuation, and what the full analytical record reveals is that this attenuation was neither uniform nor simple. It was a structural transformation: the pandemic's causal relationship with financial markets did not merely weaken during the Adaptation period but reorganized itself around a different transmission mechanism, a different indicator class, a different COVID data series, and a different temporal pattern of activation. The score decline captures the net result of this reorganization, but the reorganization itself — its specific character, its cross-metal heterogeneity, and its episodic rather than continuous operation — is the more analytically significant finding.

**What Persisted and What Was Lost.** The clearest way to characterize the Adaptation phase is through the precise accounting of what survived the transition from the Initial Shock and what did not. What survived: statistically robust Granger causality from COVID data to financial market returns, confirmed at high confidence for three of five metals with no credible reverse causality in any case; positive impulse responses consistent with the supply disruption and monetary accommodation channels for four of five metals; and episodic rolling correlation activations during acute mortality events — particularly the Delta wave — that confirm the causal channel remained capable of reactivation when pandemic severity crossed a sufficient threshold. What was lost: the broad-based, five-for-five causal reach that characterized the Initial Shock's equity market results; the near-dominant variance contribution that the S&P 500's 48.7% FEVD X-share represented; the persistent directional rolling correlation that would have been the sign of a continuously active causal channel; and the uniform response across all indicator instances within the category, replaced by the sharp cross-metal heterogeneity that differentiates platinum from gold and copper from palladium along supply chain and demand structure lines. The 9.46-point score decline is the composite of these specific losses against the backdrop of what genuinely survived.

**From Breadth to Selectivity.** The most structurally important dimension of the Adaptation phase's causal reorganization is the transition from breadth to selectivity. During the Initial Shock, COVID's causal influence was broad in two senses simultaneously: it reached across all five equity indices regardless of their compositional differences, and it operated through a single dominant channel — risk sentiment and demand destruction — that was relevant to all of them equally. During the Adaptation period, COVID's causal influence is selective in both the same two senses: it reaches only three of five metals, and it operates through the supply disruption channel, which is relevant to metals in proportion to their supply chain's geographic concentration in pandemic-affected producing regions and the industrial character of their demand. Gold escapes the causal net because its price is determined by financial flows rather than physical supply; palladium escapes it because its dominant demand driver — automotive production — was disrupted by semiconductor shortages rather than by pandemic mortality directly. Silver, platinum, and copper are caught by it because their supply chains sit in the geographic and operational zones where pandemic severity translates most directly into production constraint.

This transition from breadth to selectivity is not a degradation of the causal relationship but a specification of it. The Initial Shock's broad reach reflected a mechanism — risk sentiment — that is by nature non-selective: when pandemic uncertainty is acute and markets lack a pricing framework, every liquid asset reprices simultaneously. The Adaptation phase's selective reach reflects a mechanism — supply disruption — that is by nature specific: it operates only through the physical production chains that connect pandemic severity to commodity supply, and only for the commodities whose chains are structured in ways that make them operationally sensitive to the mortality signal the COVID death rate carries. The narrowing of COVID's causal reach from five indices to three metals is therefore an accurate representation of the pandemic's economic transmission mechanism in its mature phase rather than evidence of causal decay alone.

**From Continuity to Episodicity.** The temporal character of the Adaptation causal relationship represents an equally important structural shift from the Initial Shock. The Initial Shock's causal relationship, while not persistently signed in the rolling correlation as initially anticipated, was at minimum operating at sufficient intensity to produce five-for-five Granger significance and a near-50% S&P 500 FEVD X-share across the full 137-day window. The Adaptation causal relationship is episodic in a more fundamental sense: the rolling correlation evidence suggests it activated meaningfully during wave resurgences — the Delta wave most visibly, and likely the winter 2020–21 surge as well — and was largely dormant during the inter-wave periods when pandemic severity had declined to a level that markets treated as normalized background noise. The Granger causality results detect the average predictive content of this episodic activation across the full 536-day window, which is sufficient to achieve lag-1 significance for three metals, but the FEVD X-shares' uniformly sub-5% figures confirm that this average predictive content was economically small — a signal that was intermittently strong and persistently absent rather than continuously moderate.

**The Adaptation Phase in the Project's Cross-Phase Narrative.** The Adaptation phase's composite score and its underlying analytical structure establish two reference points for the project's cross-phase causal attenuation narrative that the subsequent Recovery and Full Period analyses will test and extend. First, they establish that COVID's causal influence survived the transition from acute crisis to endemic background condition, but only in the specific form of a supply-disruption channel operating selectively through industrial metals during acute mortality events — a much narrower survival than the breadth of the Initial Shock relationship might have suggested was possible. Second, they establish that the FEVD X-share, rather than Granger significance alone, is the most informative measure of causal magnitude at this stage of the pandemic: a relationship can be statistically robust at lag 1 while explaining only 3–4% of return variance, and that combination — significance without dominance — is the defining quantitative signature of COVID's causal role during the Adaptation period. The Adaptation phase is therefore not simply the Initial Shock with the volume turned down. It is a structurally distinct phase in which a different mechanism, a different indicator class, a different data series, and a different temporal pattern of activation together constitute a genuinely different kind of COVID → financial market relationship — one that is weaker in aggregate but more precisely specified in its mechanism, and whose specificity is the Adaptation phase's most important analytical contribution to the project's understanding of how pandemic causality evolves across a multi-year economic disruption.

### 3.3 Recovery: Total COVID Signal vs. Metal Commodity Returns

#### Economic Context

The Recovery phase (November 26, 2021 – September 14, 2022) is the shortest of the four phases at 293 days and produces the lowest composite score in the project, yet it is economically the most structurally complex — a period in which four major forces of comparable magnitude operated simultaneously, each capable of moving metal commodity markets independently and each interacting with the others in ways that make causal identification at the daily frequency genuinely difficult. Understanding what the analytical results can and cannot establish requires situating them within this compound disruption environment before interpreting any individual finding.

**The Omicron Wave: Case Surge Without Proportionate Mortality.** The Recovery phase opens almost exactly at the moment the Omicron variant achieved global dominance, and Omicron's distinctive epidemiological profile introduced a structural complication for COVID data analysis that no prior wave had created. The case surge was unprecedented in scale — confirmed daily infections reached all-time highs globally by January 2022, dwarfing every prior wave by a substantial margin — while fatality rates per infection remained substantially lower than prior waves, reflecting the partial protection conferred by vaccination and prior infection against severe disease. The result was a decoupling of the cases and deaths signals that had moved broadly together throughout the Initial Shock and Adaptation periods: case counts were signaling a pandemic of extraordinary breadth while death counts were signaling a pandemic of substantially reduced individual severity. For metal markets, the relevant signal was primarily the case count dimension — because Omicron's economic disruption operated through workforce incapacitation and China's zero-COVID policy response, both of which tracked case prevalence rather than mortality — which is why the Recovery percentage-change analysis identifies `covid_c&d%` rather than `covid_deaths%` as the leading X-variable, reversing the Adaptation phase's deaths dominance. The return of cases as the operative COVID signal is not a regression to the Initial Shock's informational structure but a phase-specific adaptation to a variant whose epidemiological profile made cases the more economically relevant pandemic severity measure.

**The Federal Reserve Tightening Cycle: A Countervailing Macro Force.** The Federal Reserve's pivot from emergency accommodation to the fastest rate hike cycle since the 1980s — beginning with the March 2022 lift-off and accelerating through the remainder of the phase — introduced a commodity market headwind that operated largely orthogonal to the COVID signal but with comparable or greater magnitude for most metals. Dollar strengthening associated with the rate differential between the Fed and other major central banks reduced the dollar-denominated price of globally traded commodities mechanically, while the repricing of inflation expectations compressed the inflation-hedge premium that had supported commodity prices broadly through the accommodation era. These tightening-driven commodity headwinds were not COVID-caused — they were the policy response to the inflation that pandemic-era stimulus had generated — but they operated simultaneously with and in the opposite direction from the supply disruption dynamics that connected COVID data to metal prices. The analytical consequence is a compression of the COVID → metal signal-to-noise ratio during the Recovery phase: any COVID-driven supply disruption support for metal prices was being partially or fully offset by tightening-driven headwinds operating at the same daily frequency, making the causal contribution of COVID data harder to detect and smaller in variance-explained terms even when genuinely present.

**The Russia-Ukraine Conflict: A Geopolitical Supply Shock Superimposed on a Pandemic One.** Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 introduced a second major supply shock to metal markets simultaneously with the tail of the Omicron wave and the beginning of Federal Reserve tightening — creating a three-way confluence of forces that is analytically the most challenging environment of the project's four phases. Russia is a significant global supplier of palladium (approximately 40% of world mine supply), nickel, aluminum, and platinum-group metals, and the sanctions, logistics disruptions, and counterparty risk repricing that followed the invasion drove sharp and idiosyncratic price spikes in these metals that had no connection to COVID data. For palladium specifically — already characterized in the Adaptation analysis as a metal whose price dynamics were dominated by automotive demand and semiconductor disruption rather than by the COVID death rate — the Russia shock introduced a third independent supply disruption narrative that further insulated the metal's return variance from COVID signal detection. For the broader metals complex, the geopolitical supply shock created a return volatility environment in which COVID-driven supply disruption signals were competing with a larger, more acute, and more immediately price-relevant geopolitical signal for the same transmission channel — physical supply constraint — making attribution of metal price movements to COVID versus geopolitical causes analytically indeterminate at the daily frequency.

**China Zero-COVID Lockdowns: The Phase's Most Direct COVID → Metal Link.** The prolonged lockdowns of Shanghai, Shenzhen, and major manufacturing and logistics centers during March–May 2022 under China's zero-COVID policy represent the Recovery phase's most direct and operationally specific COVID → metal causal pathway. Unlike the mortality-driven supply disruption channel that characterized the Adaptation phase, the China lockdown channel connected case counts directly and immediately to production capacity: lockdown triggers were explicitly case-count-based rather than mortality-based, and the lockdowns' effects on metal supply chains were near-instantaneous — shutting port operations, suspending factory production, and grounding logistics networks within days of case threshold crossings. China's role as the world's largest consumer and a major processor of industrial metals — consuming approximately 50% of global copper, significant shares of aluminum and nickel, and dominating global rare earth processing — meant that its zero-COVID policy response to Omicron created a direct causal pathway from Chinese case counts to global metal supply and demand conditions that was more operationally immediate than any supply disruption channel operating during the Adaptation period. This China-specific channel is the primary reason why covid_cases% recovers relevance as an X-variable in the Recovery percentage-change rankings — the case count, not the death count, was the variable that determined lockdown triggers and therefore the variable most directly causally upstream of Chinese industrial output and metal market conditions during this phase. It is also the channel most likely to generate the Recovery phase's detectable COVID → metal causal relationships in the Granger and VECM frameworks, and its presence — operating simultaneously with three other major market forces — defines the specific character of COVID's causal role during the project's terminal phase.

#### Why `covid_c&d` Returns as the Dominant X-Variable

The return of `covid_c&d` — the combined cases-and-deaths series used during the Initial Shock but displaced by `covid_deaths` alone during the Adaptation period — as the dominant X-variable in the Recovery phase is not a reversion to an earlier analytical state but a forward-looking adaptation to a fundamentally different epidemiological environment. It reflects the same underlying principle that drove the shift to deaths during Adaptation: the analytical framework should use the COVID data series that most accurately proxies for the pandemic's operative economic disruption mechanism during each phase. During Adaptation, that mechanism was mortality-driven supply chain disruption and monetary policy signaling, making the death rate the more reliable and less administratively contaminated signal. During the Recovery phase, the operative mechanism changed in ways that restored the informational relevance of case counts — and with it, the combined series that captures both dimensions simultaneously.

The specific epidemiological feature that drives this variable shift is Omicron's unprecedented decoupling of case counts from mortality outcomes. Every prior variant wave had maintained a reasonably stable relationship between confirmed case counts and downstream fatalities — the infection fatality rate evolved across waves as immunity accumulated and treatment protocols improved, but the directional co-movement of cases and deaths remained intact. Omicron broke this relationship structurally: by January 2022, confirmed daily case counts were reaching all-time global highs while fatality rates per infection were substantially lower than any prior wave, reflecting the partial but significant protection that vaccination and prior infection conferred against severe disease. A researcher or market participant using covid_deaths alone as the pandemic severity signal during this period would observe a death rate that was elevated but not exceptional — substantially below the winter 2020–21 surge despite cases running at multiples of that surge's peak. If deaths were the only signal being monitored, the Omicron wave might be interpreted as a moderate pandemic event. If case counts are incorporated, the picture is entirely different: the largest infectious disease case surge in recorded history, with all of the economic disruption implications that follow from population-scale workforce incapacitation regardless of individual severity.

For metal commodity markets specifically, Omicron's case surge was economically disruptive through mechanisms that tracked case counts rather than mortality. Workforce incapacitation — the primary supply disruption channel connecting pandemic severity to mining, processing, and logistics capacity — is driven by active infection prevalence, not by fatality rates. A worker who tests positive and must isolate under workplace COVID protocols represents the same operational disruption as a worker with severe disease, and under the widespread testing regimes of late 2021 and 2022, the Omicron wave generated workforce isolation requirements at a scale that directly constrained production capacity in mining operations, smelting facilities, port and logistics networks, and manufacturing plants across the metals supply chain — regardless of the attenuated mortality implications of each individual infection. The death rate, precisely because it remained relatively controlled during Omicron, was systematically understating the scale of this workforce disruption signal; the case count, precisely because it was running at historic highs, was more accurately reflecting the operational severity of the disruption even as individual medical severity declined.

The China zero-COVID policy dimension reinforces this case-count primacy further and more directly. China's lockdown trigger mechanisms were explicitly case-count-based: a case threshold crossing in a given city or district initiated the lockdown protocol regardless of the severity of cases or their mortality implications, because the zero-COVID policy was designed to eliminate transmission entirely rather than to manage mortality below a clinical threshold. The economic consequences of those lockdowns — shutdown of Shanghai port operations, suspension of manufacturing in major industrial centers, disruption of the logistics networks connecting Chinese processors to global metal supply chains — were therefore causally downstream of case counts in a direct and institutional sense, not downstream of deaths. A COVID data series that includes cases is therefore more causally complete for the Recovery phase's China channel than a deaths-only series, even setting aside the mortality-attenuation dynamics of the Omicron variant.

The candidate selection analysis confirms this variable shift empirically across both the levels and percentage-change frameworks, where `covid_c&d` and `covid_cases` together account for the majority of top-ranked pairings while `covid_deaths` retains presence but loses the dominance it held in the Adaptation rankings. This is not an analytical system selecting `covid_c&d` by coincidence or over-fitting to a short window — it is recovering the same informational structure that epidemiologists, supply chain analysts, and commodity market participants were responding to in real time during the Omicron period: a pandemic signal in which case counts had recovered economic relevance that mortality counts could no longer fully carry.

#### Key Analytical Findings

**Contemporaneous Correlation**

Spearman rank-order correlation is the appropriate method for this dataset. The COVID signal is heavily right-skewed — floored near zero with a sparse upper tail that reflects the pandemic signal's entry into a low-variance regime by late 2021 — while metal returns exhibit the fat-tailed, approximately symmetric distribution characteristic of daily commodity price changes. Spearman's resistance to these distributional departures from bivariate normality, and its robustness to the outlier observations visible across all five scatter plots, makes it the defensible choice over Pearson for this pairing. Unlike the Adaptation phase, where the five scatter profiles divided into three structurally distinct patterns that mapped onto specific transmission channels, the Recovery phase produces a single dominant scatter geometry across the full metal panel: a dense, near-horizontal cloud compressed near the y-axis floor, indicating that the COVID signal's variance had contracted sufficiently by this phase to limit the range of observable contemporaneous association regardless of whether a latent economic relationship persisted. The cross-metal differentiation visible in the Adaptation scatter profiles has collapsed by the Recovery phase into a panel-level null.

**Gold: Signal Orthogonality in a Rate-Driven Market.** The scatter shows a near-horizontal linear fit (slope: 2.14e-02), with gold percent returns spanning roughly −2.5% to +2.5% while the COVID signal clusters almost entirely below 0.5%. The point cloud is structureless — observations are uniformly distributed across the full gold return range with no systematic increase or decrease in the COVID signal at any point in the distribution. The Spearman correlation of r = 0.08 confirms negligible association. The polynomial degree summary selects degree 2 with CV R² = −0.10 and linearity_adequate = False, but the negative cross-validated R² is the operative finding: the fitted model generalizes worse than a naive mean prediction, indicating that neither linear nor quadratic structure is reliably present. The linearity_adequate = False designation is the more unusual outcome here — for most metals in the panel, linearity is adequate precisely because no structure of any kind is present; for gold, even the absence of structure is not clean enough to satisfy the linearity criterion, pointing to noise-driven degree selection rather than genuine nonlinearity.

The economic logic is consistent with this null. By late 2021, gold's price dynamics had been decisively recaptured by real interest rate movements and Federal Reserve policy expectations. The fastest rate hike cycle since the 1980s, which began with the March 2022 lift-off, drove real yields sharply higher throughout the remainder of the phase — creating a sustained headwind for gold that the COVID signal, by this point a low-amplitude and slowly varying series, had no capacity to offset or amplify at the daily return frequency. Gold's role as a monetary metal means its price is set at the margin by financial flows rather than by physical supply-demand balances, insulating it from pandemic-driven disruption channels. Whatever residual sensitivity gold retained to COVID signal variation during the Adaptation phase — where monetary accommodation dynamics provided an indirect COVID → gold link through the policy channel — had fully dissipated by Recovery as tightening replaced accommodation as the policy regime. Gold and the COVID signal were orthogonal in the Recovery phase not because the relationship weakened gradually but because the transmission mechanism connecting them had structurally inverted.

**Silver: Cubic Geometry as Volatility Artifact, Not Economic Signal.** The best-fit polynomial is cubic (y = −3.25e-03x³ + 6.29e-03x² + 5.44e-02x + 3.28e-01), descending from elevated COVID signal readings at large negative silver returns, through the dense central mass, and continuing to decline into strongly positive return territory. Geometrically, this implies that both tails of the silver return distribution coincide with lower COVID signal readings — a pattern more consistent with COVID signal compression during periods of elevated market volatility than with any directional economic relationship. When silver returns are large in either direction — reflecting acute macro disruption — the COVID signal tends to be lower, because the high-volatility episodes of the Recovery phase (the Ukraine war commodity shock, the Fed tightening repricing) were predominantly non-COVID in origin and unfolded during periods when the pandemic signal was declining or stable.

The Spearman correlation is r = 0.11, and Table 1.1.8.2 reports CV R² = −0.12 with std = 0.16. The cubic polynomial is overfitting the sparse upper-left outliers; the cross-validated model selection correctly falls back to degree 1, and that linear model explains none of the variance. The combination of a cubic best-fit polynomial in the figure, a degree-1 selection by cross-validation, and a negative CV R² is the project's diagnostic signature for a dataset in which no generalizable structure exists but the optimizer has chased noise in the sparse extremes of the distribution. This contrasts sharply with the Adaptation phase, where silver's U-shaped scatter reflected a genuine volatility coupling mechanism operating through the metal's dual industrial and precious-metal character. During Recovery, even that volatility coupling has dissolved — silver's scatter geometry reflects the absence of structure, not an alternative structure.

**Platinum: Visual Trend, Spurious Correlation.** The scatter appears, at first inspection, to show a strong positive diagonal trend — a 7th-degree polynomial traces from the lower-left to the upper-right of the plot. This demands careful interpretation, because the Spearman correlation is r = 0.09, nearly identical to gold's. The resolution lies in the scatter structure itself: the apparent trend is driven by a small number of high-leverage observations at the extremes of the platinum return distribution. The dense mass of observations clusters near zero on both axes, dominating the rank-order calculation while contributing nothing to the polynomial's shape. Table 1.1.8.2 confirms this: the cross-validated best degree is 1, CV R² = −0.03, std = 0.05. The discrepancy between the 7th-degree polynomial displayed in the figure and the degree-1 cross-validated selection is itself diagnostic — the higher-order fit is an artifact of exploratory overfitting to outliers, not evidence of structure. The platinum–COVID relationship is spurious at the contemporaneous horizon.

This finding contrasts sharply with the Adaptation phase, where platinum's scatter showed a visually coherent and economically interpretable positive directional structure — the supply disruption channel operating through South African deep-level mining, where pandemic conditions were severe enough to generate reliable positive associations between death-rate acceleration and platinum price support. The collapse of that structure by the Recovery phase has two contributing explanations. First, pandemic-era supply disruptions in South African platinum mining had normalized substantially by late 2021 as operational protocols adapted and vaccination rates among mining workforces increased. Second, automotive demand uncertainty — connected more to the semiconductor shortage and the Ukraine war's effects on European vehicle manufacturing than to COVID directly — had become platinum's dominant pricing driver by this phase, severing the supply disruption link that made the Adaptation scatter coherent. The 7th-degree polynomial is chasing the residual of a relationship that no longer exists in the contemporaneous data.

**Palladium: Supply Shock Volatility Without Pandemic Co-Movement.** Palladium's scatter spans the widest x-axis range in the panel, with daily returns running from approximately −13% to +10% — a direct consequence of the acute supply disruption introduced by the Russia-Ukraine war beginning February 2022. Despite this extreme idiosyncratic volatility, the linear fit is nearly horizontal (slope: 1.29e-02) and the Spearman correlation is r = 0.07, the weakest in the panel. The degree summary confirms a degree-1 best fit with CV R² = −0.01 and std = 0.01. That standard deviation is the most informative single figure in the palladium diagnostics: a CV R² standard deviation of 0.01 indicates that the near-zero explanatory power is stable and consistent across cross-validation folds, not a noisy average of periods of signal and periods of absence. The null result is robust rather than marginal.

The interpretation is unambiguous. Palladium's dominant pricing driver during the Recovery phase was geopolitical — Russian supply concentration, sanctions exposure, and war-related logistics disruption — a channel entirely orthogonal to the pandemic signal. Where the Adaptation phase found palladium decoupled from the COVID death rate because its automotive demand dynamics were governed by semiconductor shortages, the Recovery phase finds palladium decoupled from the COVID C&D signal because its supply dynamics were governed by a geopolitical supply shock of sufficient magnitude to absorb all available price discovery bandwidth. The COVID signal, even if it retained marginal relevance for other metals through the China zero-COVID channel, had no pathway into palladium pricing that could compete with a supply shock of this specificity and scale. Palladium is the Recovery phase's clearest example of idiosyncratic disruption completely masking any pandemic signal at the contemporaneous frequency.

**Copper: The Panel's Most Consequential Null Result.** Copper's polynomial fit equation is identical to silver's (y = −3.25e-03x³ + 6.29e-03x² + 5.44e-02x + 3.28e-01), and Table 1.1.8.2 reports matching coefficients, CV R², and AIC for both series. This identity across two series with different market structures and supply chain geographies warrants a data pipeline review to confirm the two series were not inadvertently aliased at any transformation step. The Spearman correlation is r = 0.11, matching silver exactly, and the scatter morphology is also nearly identical: a cubic descent from elevated COVID signal readings at large negative copper returns through the central mass. As with silver, the cubic shape is driven by the sparse upper-left outlier cluster rather than any generalizable non-linear structure, and CV R² = −0.12 with std = 0.16 confirms overfitting throughout.

Copper is the panel's most consequential null result because its prior for COVID sensitivity is the strongest. During the Adaptation phase, copper achieved high-confidence Granger causality at lag 1, the second-highest FEVD X-share in the group, and a positive cumulative IRF consistent with the supply disruption channel operating through Chilean and Peruvian mine disruption. Its supply chains are among the most labor-intensive in global commodity production and most geographically concentrated in regions where pandemic conditions persisted well into 2021. That this sensitivity has entirely disappeared at the contemporaneous frequency by the Recovery phase — producing a negative CV R² and a scatter profile indistinguishable from silver's structureless geometry — is strong evidence that the COVID signal's role as a copper supply disruption proxy had been fully absorbed and discounted by market participants before this phase began. The China zero-COVID channel, which the Recovery phase percentage-change rankings identify as the most likely surviving COVID → metal causal pathway, appears to operate with a lag rather than contemporaneously for copper — consistent with the Granger causality results in the following section, where lagged predictive content survives the contemporaneous null.

**Granger Causality**

The Granger causality tests examine whether lagged values of the cumulative COVID-19 C&D signal (7-day rolling average, %) carry predictive information for metal returns beyond what each metal's own return history provides, and vice versa. Both directions are tested for each metal across up to eight lags. Results are evaluated across four test statistics — F-test, chi-squared, likelihood ratio, and params-F — with the `conclusion` field reflecting whether the result meets the high-confidence threshold across the full battery. The summary table (Table 1.1.8.4) reports the dominant result for each directional pair; the metal-level tables provide the full lag-by-lag decomposition. The central question is directional asymmetry: whether the COVID signal systematically leads metal returns without the reverse being true — the minimum evidentiary standard for interpreting pandemic data as a genuine predictive input to metal pricing during this phase.The Recovery phase Granger results are the section's most analytically consequential findings precisely because they diverge from the contemporaneous correlation's uniform null. Spearman correlations of 0.07–0.11 with negative CV R² across all five metals established that no same-day relationship exists between the COVID signal and metal returns. The Granger tests reveal that this contemporaneous null coexists with genuine lagged predictive content for three of the five metals — a dissociation between zero-lag and multi-day predictive structure that is itself a substantive finding about how COVID information was absorbed by metal markets during the Recovery phase.

**Gold: No Predictive Relationship in Either Direction.** The COVID signal does not Granger-cause gold returns at any lag from 1 through 8. F-statistics peak at 2.25 at lag 2 (p = 0.11), but all four significance indicators remain False throughout, and the summary table records a `high confidence` conclusion of no causation. The reverse direction is weaker still: gold returns produce F-statistics that never exceed 1.10 across eight lags (summary: F = 1.10, p = 0.36), with all significance indicators False and an equally clean `high confidence` null. The result is bilaterally symmetric in its insignificance — neither the COVID signal nor gold's own return history carries information relevant to the other at any tested horizon.

Gold's complete decoupling from the COVID signal at the Granger level is the Recovery phase's clearest expression of the monetary mechanism displacing the pandemic mechanism as the dominant pricing channel. During the Adaptation phase, an indirect COVID → gold link operated through the monetary policy accommodation channel: rising pandemic severity reinforced near-zero rate expectations, which supported gold through real yield suppression. The Federal Reserve's pivot to aggressive tightening eliminated this channel structurally — tightening proceeded regardless of pandemic developments in the face of 40-year high inflation, meaning COVID signal variation no longer carried information about the rate path that gold markets were pricing. With the monetary accommodation channel closed and gold's supply chain not materially exposed to pandemic disruption, no transmission pathway remained through which lagged COVID data could predict gold returns. The result is not attenuation of a prior relationship but its architectural elimination.

**Silver: Unidirectional Causation from COVID Signal to Silver.** Silver produces the panel's first directional finding and one that qualitatively reframes the contemporaneous null. At lag 4, the COVID → silver direction yields F = 3.37, p = 0.01, with all four significance indicators True — `f_significant`, `chi2_significant`, `lrtest_significant`, and `params_f_significant` — and a `high confidence` conclusion. The four-test unanimity is the strongest possible outcome the framework can produce: the predictive relationship is robust to the specific inferential approach used to detect it rather than being an artifact of any single test's assumptions. At lag 2, a partial signal emerges where `chi2_significant` and `lrtest_significant` are True while `f_significant` and `params_f_significant` remain False, accompanied by a non-normal residuals or `insufficient sample size` flag. This partial lag-2 result is best treated as a diagnostic warning rather than a secondary confirmation: distributional violations at that specific lag make the chi-squared and likelihood ratio statistics unreliable, and the F-test's non-significance at the same lag is the more robust finding.

The reverse direction — silver prices Granger-causing the COVID signal — produces no significant result at any lag across the full eight-lag window (summary: F = 1.43, p = 0.22, all significance indicators False, `high confidence`). The relationship is strictly unidirectional: lagged COVID signal values carry predictive information for silver returns, but silver prices carry none for subsequent COVID signal readings. This asymmetry is the economically coherent direction and its presence at lag 4 rather than lag 1 is substantively informative. During the Adaptation phase, the COVID → silver predictive relationship operated at lag 1 — a one-day processing horizon consistent with supply disruption signals being priced rapidly into a liquid market. The Recovery phase's shift to lag 4 indicates a slower and more diffuse information absorption process, consistent with two concurrent changes: the COVID signal's variance compression making each daily increment less salient to market participants, and the China zero-COVID lockdown channel — which drove the Recovery phase's most direct COVID → commodity causal pathway — operating through a slower sequence of case threshold crossings, lockdown announcements, and production disruption realizations that took multiple trading days to reach silver pricing rather than being instantaneously incorporated.

**Platinum: Bidirectional Granger Causation.** Platinum is the only metal in the panel to show statistically significant Granger causation in both directions and the most analytically complex result in this section. In the COVID → platinum direction, lag 2 yields F = 4.50, p = 0.01, with all four significance indicators True and a `high confidence` conclusion. Lag 1 is not significant (F = 1.98, p = 0.16), establishing that the predictive relationship emerges with a two-day delay — slower than the Adaptation phase's lag-1 platinum result, consistent with the same signal compression dynamics that pushed the silver lag from 1 to 4. In the platinum → COVID direction, lag 1 yields F = 5.07, p = 0.03, with all four indicators True and a `high confidence` conclusion. This is the only instance in the panel where a metal's returns significantly Granger-cause the COVID signal, and the lag-1 structure means platinum returns anticipate subsequent COVID signal readings by a full trading day.

The bidirectional result demands careful interpretation. Granger causality establishes predictive precedence, not structural causation, and no plausible mechanism connects daily platinum price movements to pandemic mortality or case outcomes. The economically coherent reading is that platinum and the COVID signal shared a common driver during the Recovery phase that manifested with different transmission delays in each series — most plausibly, global industrial activity and supply chain conditions, which simultaneously affected both platinum's autocatalyst and industrial demand and the operational conditions that generated the case and death counts entering the COVID signal. Platinum markets, as deep and institutionally active commodity markets, priced changes in this common driver faster than the epidemiological reporting cycle could capture them in the COVID series — producing a platinum-leads-COVID structure at lag 1 that reflects information speed differences rather than any causal influence of prices on pandemic outcomes.

The COVID → platinum direction at lag 2 is separately interpretable and not contradicted by the reverse result. Two trading days after a COVID signal movement, platinum prices respond in a way that the platinum return's own history does not predict — consistent with a delayed supply chain effect, a position adjustment by institutional participants incorporating pandemic information into autocatalyst demand models, or both. The bidirectional structure is therefore not a logical contradiction but a reflection of two overlapping mechanisms operating at different horizons: a fast-moving common driver that platinum prices incorporate before the COVID signal does (lag 1, reverse direction), and a slower supply disruption or demand channel through which the COVID signal itself — once updated — carries predictive content for platinum (lag 2, forward direction).

**Palladium: No Predictive Relationship in Either Direction.** Palladium shows no Granger causation in either direction across all tested lags. The COVID → palladium direction produces F-statistics that decline monotonically from lag 1 (F = 2.25, p = 0.14) through lag 8 (F = 0.99, p = 0.44), with all significance indicators consistently False throughout. The reverse direction is similarly null, with F-statistics declining monotonically from lag 1 (F = 1.70, p = 0.19) through lag 8 (F = 0.39, p = 0.92). Both directions carry `high confidence` conclusions of no causation. No lag in either direction produces even a partial signal from any individual test statistic.

The monotonic decline in F-statistics across both directions is the most structurally informative feature of the palladium results. In a dataset where some causal content is present but noisy, F-statistics fluctuate across lags rather than declining smoothly — the peak lag emerges from a pattern of elevated but variable statistics. The clean monotonic descent observed for both palladium directions is the statistical signature of genuine orthogonality: a signal that was never present at any horizon, diminishing only because longer lags consume degrees of freedom without adding predictive content. This is categorically different from the silver and copper profiles, where the lag-4 peak is distinguishable from the surrounding lag structure, and confirms that palladium and the COVID signal were genuinely decoupled during the Recovery phase rather than weakly or intermittently connected.

The interpretation is direct. Palladium's dominant pricing variable during the Recovery phase was the Russia-Ukraine war's supply disruption — a geopolitical channel that operated entirely separately from the pandemic and that generated the extreme return volatility visible in palladium's scatter plot (x-axis range: −13% to +10%). The COVID signal, even through the China zero-COVID channel that provided the Recovery phase's most direct pandemic-to-commodity causal pathway, had no purchase on palladium pricing because the war supply shock absorbed the full bandwidth of supply disruption pricing for this metal. A COVID signal that could move silver and copper through lagged supply chain mechanisms was simply irrelevant to a metal whose supply outlook was being repriced daily against Russian sanctions exposure and war logistics disruption.

**Copper: Unidirectional Causation from COVID Signal to Copper.** Copper's Granger causality results are identical to silver's across all lags and all test statistics. At lag 4, COVID → copper yields F = 3.37, p = 0.01, with all four significance indicators True and a `high confidence` conclusion. At lag 2, the same partial signal appears — `chi2_significant` and `lrtest_significant` True, `f_significant` and `params_f_significant` False — with the same `non-normal residuals` or `insufficient sample size` flag, subject to the same interpretive reservation applied to silver's lag-2 result. The reverse direction is entirely null across eight lags (summary: F = 1.43, p = 0.22, all indicators False, `high confidence`), matching silver exactly. The COVID signal Granger-causes copper; copper does not Granger-cause the COVID signal.

The economic interpretation of the lag-4 copper result is more informative when read against the Adaptation phase's lag-1 finding. During Adaptation, the COVID → copper predictive relationship operated within a single trading day — supply disruption signals from South American producing regions were being priced into copper returns at daily frequency by institutionally sophisticated participants tracking pandemic conditions in Chile and Peru continuously. The Recovery phase's shift to lag 4 indicates that this rapid-response supply disruption pricing mechanism had been substantially replaced by a slower-moving information channel. The most plausible candidate is the China zero-COVID lockdown pathway: case threshold crossings in Chinese industrial cities triggered lockdown announcements, which then propagated through port operations, factory production schedules, and logistics networks over multiple days before their full implications for copper demand and inventory flows were reflected in spot prices. A four-day processing lag is economically coherent for a channel that operates through institutional and logistical response sequences rather than through immediate market repricing of supply disruption expectations.

The exact duplication of silver and copper results across contemporaneous correlation, polynomial diagnostics, and Granger tests — identical F-statistics, p-values, CV R², and AIC throughout — reinforces the data pipeline concern raised in the contemporaneous section and should be treated as a verification priority before these results are relied upon for publication. If the duplication reflects a genuine shared signal structure rather than a pipeline artifact, it would imply that the COVID C&D series carries identical lag-4 predictive content for silver and copper through a common mechanism — most plausibly the China zero-COVID channel, which disrupted both metals' supply and demand conditions simultaneously and through the same institutional trigger sequence. If it reflects aliasing, the individual-series results are unreliable and require recomputation from the source series.
**The Overall Causal Picture.** The Granger results materially qualify the contemporaneous null findings. Across five metals, the COVID signal Granger-causes three — silver, platinum, and copper — while no metal Granger-causes the COVID signal except platinum. The predictive relationship is therefore predominantly unidirectional: lagged pandemic signal movements carry information about subsequent metal returns, even when same-day co-movement is negligible. This dissociation between contemporaneous correlation and lagged predictive power is consistent with a market environment in which COVID information was priced with a delay during the Recovery phase, as investors weighted pandemic developments against the dominant macro drivers of the period before adjusting commodity positions.

**The Overall Causal Picture.** The Granger results materially qualify the contemporaneous null established in the prior section. Across five metals, the COVID signal Granger-causes three — silver, platinum, and copper — while no metal Granger-causes the COVID signal except platinum, and platinum's reverse causation is interpretable as information speed differential rather than structural causation. The predictive relationships are predominantly unidirectional and operate with multi-day lags: lag 4 for silver and copper, lag 2 for platinum in the forward direction. This lag structure is longer than the Adaptation phase's lag-1 results for the same three metals, reflecting the COVID signal's variance compression during the Recovery phase and the shift from a mortality-driven supply disruption channel — which metal markets had learned to price rapidly — to a case-count-driven China lockdown channel that propagated through institutional and logistical response sequences over multiple trading days before reaching spot prices. The dissociation between contemporaneous nulls and lagged significance is the Recovery phase's defining causal signature: COVID information was present in metal pricing during this phase, but processed slowly and indirectly, in a market environment where three competing macro forces — Federal Reserve tightening, the Ukraine war, and the Omicron-driven China lockdown cycle — were simultaneously claiming the same transmission channels through which the pandemic signal had to travel.

**VAR/VECM Dynamics**

All five metal pairs are modeled using VECM(lag = 7, rank = 1), with BIC selecting lag 8 — VECM lag 7 — uniformly across the group. The cointegration rank of 2 is confirmed by trace tests for all five pairs; max-eigenvalue tests agree for platinum and palladium, while gold, silver, and copper rely on the trace result where the two tests diverge. Despite the mixed test agreement, the VECM specification is applied consistently throughout on the basis of the trace result, which is the more conservative and widely accepted criterion in applied cointegration analysis. All five metal return series are stationary (ADF p = 0.000 throughout) while the COVID C&D signal is non-stationary (ADF = −2.10, p = 0.24) — the asymmetric stationarity that motivates the VECM framework. Adjustment speeds for the COVID signal (x) are uniformly near zero across all five pairs (ranging from −0.000166 for palladium to 0.000879 for platinum), meaning the pandemic signal does not error-correct toward the cointegrating equilibrium — it evolves independently. All metal return series (y) carry larger and more variable adjustment speeds, ranging from 0.052 for silver and copper to 0.886 for palladium. All five models return infinite error correction half-lives, indicating that while the cointegrating relationships exist, none generates mean reversion within the 30-period IRF horizon. Mean reversion is confirmed for gold and palladium; it is not confirmed for silver, platinum, and copper, where the alpha sign diagnostic flags potentially diverging rather than equilibrating dynamics.

The IRF sign is uniformly positive across all five metals — a one-standard-deviation shock to the COVID C&D signal produces a net positive cumulative response in each metal's return over the 30-period horizon. This directional consistency is the VAR/VECM framework's most structurally informative panel-level result: across metals with materially different supply chain geographies, demand structures, and Granger causality profiles, COVID signal shocks are associated with positive rather than negative metal price impulses throughout the Recovery phase. The positive sign is consistent with the supply disruption interpretation — rising cumulative COVID signal accelerations constrained supply through workforce incapacitation and the China zero-COVID lockdown mechanism, generating upward price pressure that outweighed any concurrent demand destruction signal. It is also consistent with the contemporaneous correlation's uniformly positive (if statistically negligible) Spearman coefficients, providing the VAR/VECM framework's confirmation that the direction of the COVID → metal relationship, however small in magnitude, was not reversed during the Recovery phase.

**Gold: Mean-Reverting System, Moderate FEVD X-Share.** Gold's IRF peaks at lag 1 with a magnitude of 3.27, making it the fastest-peaking response in the panel. The x_shock_y_response values oscillate across the 30-period horizon — reaching 3.27 at period 1, falling to −0.76 at period 3, recovering to 1.15 at period 4, and continuing in this oscillating pattern without a clear directional trend — producing a cumulative x→y response of 6.57. The oscillation pattern is structurally distinct from the more steadily positive profiles observed for platinum and palladium, suggesting that gold's response to a COVID shock is rapid but not sustained: the initial positive impulse partially reverses within two periods and then fluctuates around a modest positive cumulative level. Gold is the only metal in the panel classified as `is_stable = True` alongside palladium in the summary table, and the mean reversion confirmation for gold is the VAR/VECM framework's acknowledgment that the error correction dynamics are better-conditioned for this pair than for silver, platinum, or copper.

The FEVD X-share for gold is 6.94%, rising from 0.04 at period 1 to 0.05 by period 2 and stabilizing in that range through period 23 before a late-window drift to 0.07 by period 30. The gradual and nearly monotonic ascent of the X-share — with no inflection point or leveling-off visible within the 31-period horizon — is the FEVD's confirmation that the COVID signal's variance contribution to gold forecasts was still accumulating at period 30 rather than having reached a stable equilibrium. The 6.94% figure is therefore a lower bound estimate of COVID's eventual variance contribution to gold under this VECM specification. The gold → COVID FEVD (`x_variance_from_y`) is consistently 0.00 across all periods, confirming the Granger causality section's finding that gold returns carry no information about future COVID signal dynamics — the FEVD's independent corroboration of that directional null.

Despite the moderate FEVD X-share, the gold Granger result was a clean null. The FEVD and Granger findings are not contradictory — they measure different things. A VECM FEVD can attribute meaningful forecast variance to a shock variable even when the Granger test finds no significant lagged predictive content, because the FEVD operates within a system that imposes the cointegrating structure identified by the trace test, which creates a long-run linkage between the two series that influences variance decomposition even in the absence of short-run Granger predictability. The gold FEVD X-share of 6.94% reflects the cointegrating equilibrium's influence on the variance decomposition rather than short-run daily predictive content — and should be interpreted accordingly.

**Silver: Rapid Peak, Attenuating Response, Moderate FEVD X-Share.** Silver's IRF peaks at lag 2 with a magnitude of 5.02 — the joint-highest period-1 observation alongside copper, which shares an identical IRF profile. The x_shock_y_response column shows an initial strong positive response (4.11 at period 1, 5.02 at period 2), followed by a sharp reversal to −3.16 at period 3, and then a series of oscillating positive and negative values that diminish in amplitude across the mid-horizon before stabilizing near zero in the later periods. The cumulative x→y response of 5.40 is the lowest positive cumulative total in the panel, reflecting the partial offset between the strong initial positive impulse and the negative reversal at period 3. Silver's `is_stable = False` classification and unconfirmed mean reversion flag the VECM's error correction dynamics as less reliable for this pair, suggesting that the oscillating IRF profile may reflect estimation instability rather than a genuine economic reversal pattern.

The FEVD X-share for silver is 7.09%, tracking almost identically to gold's profile across the full 31-period horizon — rising from 0.02 at period 1 to 0.04 by period 2 through 3, stabilizing in the 0.05–0.06 range through period 21, and drifting to 0.07 by period 30. The silver and copper FEVD profiles are exact duplicates, consistent with the pipeline duplication concern raised throughout this section. The `x_variance_from_y` column for silver shows a very slow drift from 1.00 at period 0 to 0.99 by period 2, stabilizing at 0.99 through the later periods — indicating that silver returns explain an extremely small but nonzero fraction of COVID signal forecast variance across the horizon, consistent with the weak shared dynamics captured by the VECM's cointegrating structure.

**Platinum: Strongest FEVD X-Share, Unconfirmed Mean Reversion.** Platinum produces the panel's highest FEVD X-share at 7.89% and the panel's highest IRF peak magnitude at 5.32 (period 1). The x_shock_y_response profile shows a strong immediate response at period 1 (5.32), declining sharply to 1.49 at period 2 and turning negative at period 3 (−0.77), then recovering to positive territory by period 6 (2.59) and oscillating through the remainder of the horizon without a clear decaying trend. The cumulative x→y response of 20.06 is the second highest in the panel after palladium, and substantially above silver and copper's 5.40. The large cumulative total relative to the modest oscillating period-by-period values reflects the non-dissipating character of the platinum IRF — the response does not decay toward zero across the 30-period horizon, consistent with the unconfirmed mean reversion flag and the infinite half-life.

The FEVD X-share's evolution is the slowest and most gradual of any metal in the panel: rising from 0.03 at periods 1 through 5, to 0.04 by period 6, 0.05 by period 11, 0.06 by period 15, 0.07 by period 20, and reaching 0.08 by period 27 before a final drift to 0.08 at period 30. This steady, nearly linear ascent — with no sign of leveling off at the 31-period horizon — makes platinum's FEVD X-share the most clearly lower-bounded of any metal: the 7.89% figure is the most conservative estimate among all five metals because the accumulation rate at period 30 is still positive and approximately constant. The platinum x_variance_from_y column is uniformly 1.00 through period 27, then declines fractionally to 0.99 at periods 28–30 — effectively zero platinum return contribution to COVID signal forecast variance throughout the horizon. This is the FEVD's confirmation of the directional asymmetry established by the Granger tests: the COVID signal influences platinum, but platinum does not influence the COVID signal at the variance decomposition level, despite the reverse Granger causality finding at lag 1. The bidirectional Granger result was interpreted as an information speed differential rather than structural reverse causation; the FEVD confirms that interpretation by showing zero platinum → COVID variance contribution at the system level.

**Palladium: Delayed Peak, Largest Cumulative IRF, Lowest FEVD X-Share.** Palladium presents the most structurally anomalous VAR/VECM profile in the panel. The IRF peaks at lag 11 — the latest peak of any metal by a margin of nine periods — with a magnitude of 4.44. The x_shock_y_response column shows a distinctive trajectory: an initial near-zero response at period 1 (0.28), a sharp negative dip from periods 2 through 5 (reaching −3.88 at period 3 and −1.98 at period 5), then a recovery through period 6 (4.26) and a sustained positive profile through the remainder of the horizon, reaching the peak of 4.44 at period 11 and maintaining positive values of 3–4 units through period 30. The cumulative x→y response of 50.12 is the largest in the panel by a factor of approximately 2.5 relative to platinum's 20.06, driven by the sustained positive plateau from period 6 onward rather than by any single large impulse.

This profile is paradoxical relative to the Granger causality null for palladium. The Granger test found no significant COVID → palladium predictive content at any lag from 1 through 8, yet the VECM IRF shows a large positive cumulative response with a sustained plateau from period 6 through 30. The resolution lies in the methodological differences between Granger testing and VECM impulse response analysis. The Granger test searches for short-run predictive content at specific lags up to lag 8; the VECM IRF propagates shocks through the full system dynamics including the cointegrating equilibrium structure over a 30-period horizon. The palladium result is consistent with a relationship that has no short-run daily predictive structure detectable by a lag-by-lag Granger test but is present at longer horizons through the cointegrating equilibrium that the VECM imposes. The lag-11 peak falls outside the Granger test's lag window entirely, which explains the apparent contradiction: the VECM is detecting a slow-building response that the Granger framework's eight-lag window cannot reach.

The FEVD X-share tells a different and more cautionary story. The `x_variance_from_y` column is uniformly 1.00 across all 31 periods, confirming zero palladium return contribution to COVID signal forecast variance throughout. The combination of a large cumulative IRF and a low FEVD X-share is a diagnostic pattern associated with high baseline variance in the y series: when palladium's own return shocks are extremely large — as they were during the Russia-Ukraine supply disruption — the FEVD denominates COVID's contribution against a very large total forecast variance, making the percentage share small even when the absolute impulse response is substantial. The cumulative IRF of 50.12 does not contradict the FEVD's 2.97% — it confirms that COVID shocks produced a real but proportionally minor contribution to a metal whose return variance was dominated by a far larger and more idiosyncratic geopolitical supply shock throughout the Recovery phase.

**Lag Correlation**

The lag correlation analysis tests whether the COVID C&D signal systematically leads or lags metal returns across a ±24-day window, providing a temporal ordering complement to the Granger causality results. A peak lag in the positive region — meaning the COVID signal at time t is most strongly correlated with a metal's return at time t+k for some positive k — would confirm that pandemic data anticipated metal market movements rather than merely synchronizing with them or following them. The summary table (Table 1.1.8.8) reports Spearman same-day correlations, peak lag locations, peak r-values, Bonferroni-corrected p-values, and the r_improvement metric — the difference between the peak lag r-value and the same-day r-value — as the primary cross-metal comparanda. No metal achieves a significant same-day correlation (`significant = False` throughout), and no metal achieves a significant peak after Bonferroni correction (`significant_peak = False` throughout, all p_peak_bonferroni = 1.00). The lag correlation results constitute a uniform null across the panel in terms of statistical significance, but the profile shapes, peak locations, and r_improvement values carry meaningful cross-metal structure that warrants careful reading.

**The Dominant Profile Shape: Positive-Right Asymmetry.** The most structurally important feature of the five individual lag correlation charts is a shared profile asymmetry that distinguishes the Recovery phase lag results from the Adaptation phase's flat, featureless noise floors. In four of five metals — gold, silver, palladium, and copper — the lag correlation curve is systematically higher in the positive-lag half (COVID leads metal) than in the negative-lag half (metal leads COVID), producing a rightward-biased profile that rises from a slightly negative base in the far negative-lag region, crosses zero near lag 0, and sustains a modest positive plateau across the full positive-lag range before gently declining at the far right. This asymmetry is not the result of any individual large positive observation at a specific lag — the profiles are smooth and gradual rather than peaked — but of a systematic tendency for the COVID signal to have a weak positive association with metal returns at all positive lags simultaneously, while the negative-lag region carries a weak negative association.

The positive-right asymmetry is the lag correlation's most direct evidence that the COVID signal functioned as a leading rather than lagging variable for these four metals, even though the leading relationship is too weak to survive Bonferroni correction. The profile shape — negative on the left, near-zero at zero, positive and sustained on the right — is the textbook signature of a variable that leads its counterpart: it is positively correlated with the future values of the other series and negatively correlated with its own past. In a dataset where the COVID signal's variance had compressed dramatically by March 2022 and competing macro forces were operating simultaneously, the emergence of even a weak positive-right asymmetry across four of five metals is a meaningful structural finding about the direction of the COVID–metal relationship at the aggregate window frequency.

**Gold: Widest Positive-Lag Range, Latest Peak.** Gold's lag correlation (Figure 1.1.3.6) peaks at lag +12 with r = 0.155 — the highest peak r-value in the panel and the latest positive peak. The profile rises smoothly from approximately r = −0.08 at lag −24, crosses zero between lags −10 and −5, reaches approximately r = 0.10 at lag 0, and continues rising to the peak of 0.155 at lag +12 before declining gradually to approximately r = 0.10 at lag +24. The r_improvement of 0.31 is the smallest in absolute terms when compared to platinum's 0.52, but the smoothness and breadth of the positive plateau make it the most visually coherent positive-right profile of the four asymmetric metals.

The peak at lag +12 sits well outside the Granger test's lag window of 1 through 8, which is directly relevant to the Granger null for gold established in the earlier section. The Granger test found no significant COVID → gold predictive content at any lag up to 8, and the lag correlation confirms that the positive-right structure for gold is concentrated at lags 10–15 — a horizon the Granger framework did not test. This does not constitute evidence of COVID → gold causality at lag 12, because the peak r of 0.155 falls far below the ±0.30 significance threshold and the Bonferroni-corrected p-value of 1.00 confirms no significance at any lag. It does suggest, however, that the COVID signal's relationship with gold — if any residual relationship existed during the Recovery phase — operated at a longer temporal horizon than the Granger framework's eight-lag window could detect, and at an amplitude too small to clear any conventional significance standard. A case count that is partly determined by how many tests are being administered — and administrative decisions about who qualifies for testing — is a degraded signal for financial market analysis relative to one that reflects actual pandemic severity. Fatality counts, while subject to their own reporting lags and attribution conventions, were substantially harder to inflate through expanded testing and substantially more directly connected to the economic disruption channels that commodity and currency markets were pricing during the Adaptation period: labor force incapacitation, healthcare system strain, behavioral restriction, and the severity of supply chain disruption. The candidate selection analysis recovers this informational pivot empirically: `covid_deaths` appears in seven of the top ten rows in both the Adaptation levels and percentage-change analyses, confirming that the shift to fatality data is robust across two independent analytical frameworks rather than being a specification-dependent artifact.

The vaccine rollout beginning in December 2020 reinforced and deepened the deaths-dominance during Adaptation in a way that made the variable shift structurally self-sustaining rather than merely convenient. As vaccination rates rose through spring 2021, the previously stable relationship between confirmed case counts and downstream fatalities weakened decisively: vaccinated populations continued generating positive tests — particularly under expanded surveillance testing regimes — but at substantially lower fatality rates than unvaccinated populations. A rising case count in a highly vaccinated population carried fundamentally different economic implications than the same case count in an unvaccinated population, and a market participant using raw case counts as a severity proxy would systematically misread the pandemic's economic implications from late 2020 onward. The death rate, by contrast, remained a more consistent severity measure across vaccination status strata — continuing to capture the fraction of the population experiencing severe enough illness to generate the supply chain disruption, mobility restriction, and healthcare system strain that were the Adaptation period's dominant COVID → economic transmission mechanisms.

The return of `covid_c&d` as the leading X-variable in the Recovery phase is not a regression to the Initial Shock's informational structure but a forward-looking adaptation to the Omicron variant's specific epidemiological properties, which were structurally unlike every prior wave in one decisive respect: the decoupling of case counts from mortality outcomes at an unprecedented scale. By January 2022, confirmed daily infections were reaching all-time global highs while fatality rates per infection remained substantially lower than any prior wave, reflecting the partial but significant protection that vaccination and prior infection conferred against severe disease. A researcher or market participant relying on `covid_deaths` alone during this period would observe a death rate that was elevated but not exceptional — substantially below the winter 2020–21 surge despite cases running at multiples of that surge's peak — and would therefore systematically underestimate the economic disruption implications of the Omicron wave. The disruption that mattered for commodity and currency markets during Omicron operated primarily through workforce incapacitation and the China zero-COVID policy response, both of which tracked case prevalence rather than mortality. A worker isolating under a positive test represented the same supply chain disruption as a worker with severe disease; a Chinese city triggering lockdown protocols based on a case threshold represented the same disruption to global industrial output regardless of the severity of individual infections within it. Incorporating case counts back into the dominant X-variable was therefore not a methodological inconsistency but a rational response to a variant whose economic footprint was disproportionately case-driven rather than mortality-driven.

The full-period selection of raw `covid_deaths` without smoothing or accumulation stands apart from all three phase selections and reflects a different optimization criterion: temporal persistence rather than phase-specific signal strength. The raw daily death percentage change is the noisiest and least transformed representation of pandemic mortality in the project's variable set, and its selection as the full-period leader means it outperformed every smoothed, cumulated, and combined alternative across the full 966-day window when tested against exchange rate returns. This is interpretable as evidence that currency markets — operating continuously across global time zones at the highest institutional participation levels of any asset class — were processing COVID mortality data at the daily reporting frequency with sufficient speed and precision that smoothing the signal introduced lag rather than clarity. The rolling average and cumulative transforms, designed to reduce noise and capture trajectory rather than daily fluctuation, were less predictive for exchange rates at the full-period frequency precisely because exchange rate markets had already incorporated the smoothing function internally: institutional participants were tracking rolling mortality trends in real time, making the raw daily death change the marginal new information that moved prices rather than the smoothed trajectory that had already been priced. The full-period metric selection is therefore the project's empirical recovery of a market-specific information processing speed that distinguished currency markets from the equity and commodity markets that required smoothed inputs to generate their strongest causal signals.

### 4.3 The Shift in the Dominant Economic Indicator

The asset class most strongly linked to COVID data changed across phases in a sequence that was not random but traced the pandemic's shifting economic transmission mechanism with precision — each dominant indicator reflecting the specific channel through which pandemic severity was reaching financial market prices at that point in the pandemic's evolution.

| Phase | Dominant Y-Variable | Primary Transmission Channel | Channel Speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initial Shock | `stk_mkt` (equities) | Risk sentiment, demand destruction, earnings expectations | Hours to days |
| Adaptation | `metals` (commodities) | Supply chain disruption, monetary accommodation, inflation expectations | Days to weeks |
| Recovery | `metals` (commodities) | China zero-COVID lockdowns, geopolitical supply premium | Days to weeks |
| Full Period | `exchg_rts` (currencies) | Monetary policy divergence, safe-haven dynamics, trade flow disruption | Weeks to months |

The progression maps directly onto a structural feature of pandemic economics that distinguishes COVID-19 from prior financial crises: its simultaneous operation as a demand shock, a supply shock, and a monetary policy shock across different phases and through different asset class channels, with the speed of each channel's transmission determining both which indicator class dominated and how long the relationship persisted before being absorbed into market pricing as a background condition.

**Equities as the Initial Shock's Primary Receptor.** Equity markets were the natural first receptor of the pandemic's economic signal during the Initial Shock because they are the most liquid, continuously repriced, and institutionally dominant asset class for absorbing changes in aggregate economic expectations. The mechanism was direct and fast: rising COVID case and death counts in February–March 2020 generated immediate revisions to earnings expectations across nearly every sector simultaneously, drove risk premium repricing as the probability of a severe and prolonged recession was rapidly updated, and triggered the mechanical deleveraging and margin call dynamics of a liquidity-driven market stress event. This risk sentiment channel operated at the daily return frequency — COVID data arriving in the morning was in equity prices by the close — and it operated broadly, reaching all five major equity indices without meaningful heterogeneity in direction, only in magnitude and lag. Its defining feature was speed: no other transmission channel operates at the same daily frequency with the same institutional reach across the full breadth of the equity market. Its defining limitation was equally characteristic of fast-moving channels: it priced in and closed rapidly. By mid-2020, equity markets had established a pricing framework for pandemic risk, and the marginal predictive content of daily COVID data for equity returns collapsed accordingly.

**Metals as the Adaptation and Recovery Indicator.** The emergence of metals as the dominant COVID-sensitive indicator class during the Adaptation period reflects a fundamental reorientation of the pandemic's financial market transmission mechanism from the demand side to the supply side. As equity markets normalized their relationship with pandemic data, the economic channel that remained active and structurally important was the disruption of physical supply chains connecting the extraction, processing, and delivery of raw materials to their end markets. Metals sit at the intersection of this supply-side channel more directly than any other liquid asset class — their prices are determined at the margin by the physical availability of the commodity, and that availability was being continuously disrupted by pandemic conditions in the geographically concentrated producing regions of South America, South Africa, and eventually China. The supply disruption channel is structurally slower to materialize than the sentiment channel — it requires the accumulation of pandemic conditions across production networks, logistics chains, and labor markets over days to weeks before it generates statistically detectable price effects — but it is correspondingly more durable once established, because the physical consequences of workforce incapacitation and logistics disruption cannot be priced away by a single repricing event the way that risk sentiment can.

The persistence of metals as the dominant class across both the Adaptation and Recovery phases — despite the Recovery phase's additional competing forces of Federal Reserve tightening, the Ukraine war supply shock, and the Omicron variant's case-mortality decoupling — confirms that the supply disruption channel was more structurally robust than the sentiment channel it replaced. The specific mechanism evolved between the two phases: Adaptation was organized around mortality-driven mine and logistics disruption in South America and South Africa, while Recovery was organized around the China zero-COVID lockdown mechanism that translated case-count thresholds directly into industrial production shutdowns. But the indicator class remained the same because the underlying economic logic remained the same — pandemic severity affecting the physical availability of industrial raw materials in ways that metal spot prices incorporated with a multi-day processing lag.

**Exchange Rates as the Full-Period Residual.** The identification of exchange rates as the dominant full-period indicator class is the progression's most theoretically consequential finding. Currency markets led the full-period percentage-change rankings not because they showed the strongest COVID sensitivity at any specific point in the pandemic — equities were more COVID-sensitive during the Initial Shock and metals were more COVID-sensitive during the Adaptation phase — but because the exchange rate channel was the only one active across all three phases simultaneously, sustained by mechanisms that operated at temporal frequencies too slow to close within any single-phase window.

The monetary policy divergence channel — the most important sustained COVID → exchange rate mechanism — operated through the medium-run horizon of central bank reaction function updating: weeks to months rather than the hours-to-days of equity sentiment or the days-to-weeks of commodity supply disruption. This slower operational frequency made it resistant to the market habituation that closed the faster channels. Equity sentiment repriced within a single-phase because institutional participants developed a stable framework for valuing pandemic risk quickly. The supply disruption channel narrowed across the Recovery phase as pandemic-era disruptions normalized and competing geopolitical supply shocks absorbed the same transmission bandwidth. But monetary policy divergence across major currency areas remained genuinely uncertain and COVID-conditional throughout the full 966 days — each new wave generated real uncertainty about whether central banks would need to extend accommodation, delay normalization, or respond asymmetrically across currency areas, and currency markets priced that uncertainty continuously rather than discounting it as a known background condition.

The trade flow and current account channel added a further layer of persistence at an even longer temporal frequency. Accumulated mortality differentials across major trading economies fed into current account imbalances, productivity differentials, and fiscal sustainability assessments on a quarterly-to-annual cycle that exchange rate markets incorporated prospectively but that required the full pandemic timeline to accumulate to a scale visible in the econometric results. The full-period analysis, precisely because it spans all 966 days, is the only specification long enough to detect this slower-moving channel alongside the faster mechanisms — and the exchange rate class's leadership of the full-period rankings is in part a reflection of being the only indicator category whose dominant transmission mechanism operates at the temporal scale that a 966-day pooled analysis is best positioned to recover.

**The Sequence as a Structural Finding.** Read as a complete arc, the three-phase indicator rotation — equities to metals to currencies — is not a coincidence of variable selection or a statistical artifact of the composite scoring methodology. It is the project's empirical recovery of a well-defined economic structure: pandemic shocks propagate through financial markets in a specific order determined by each transmission channel's operational speed and institutional reach, from the fastest and most liquid (equity sentiment), through the intermediate and physically grounded (commodity supply disruption), to the slowest and most structurally persistent (monetary policy divergence and trade flow adjustment). Each channel dominates during the phase whose temporal scale matches its transmission speed, and each closes — or is displaced by competing forces — as the pandemic evolves past the conditions that sustained it. The indicator rotation is the project's most direct evidence that COVID-19's financial market footprint was not a single undifferentiated shock but a structured sequence of transmission mechanisms that activated, peaked, and attenuated in a specific order across the pandemic's complete economic timeline.

### 4.4 The Role of Smoothing Transformations

The project's systematic variation of COVID data transformations across notebooks — cumulative accumulation, 7-day rolling average, both combined, or neither — was not a design convenience but an analytical feature whose cross-phase variation reveals how different pandemic signals were being processed by different markets at different points in the pandemic's evolution. The transformation that dominates each phase's top rankings encodes information about the temporal frequency and informational structure of the COVID → market relationship during that phase, and the progression across phases is itself a finding about how pandemic information was being absorbed and acted upon by financial market participants.

**Cumulative Accumulation.** The cumulative transform (`xcml`) — which converts daily case and death counts into their running total — consistently improves composite scores during the Initial Shock and Adaptation phases, and the economic logic is precise. During a phase in which the pandemic is actively expanding and its ultimate economic consequences remain uncertain, the accumulated stock of cases and deaths is more relevant to asset pricing than the daily flow increment for two reinforcing reasons. First, the cumulative total proxies for the pandemic's accumulated damage to productive capacity, labor force availability, and healthcare system resilience — the economic consequences that financial markets are attempting to price — more completely than any single day's count, which is subject to reporting noise, testing capacity constraints, and day-of-week periodicity. Second, the cumulative total is what rational forward-looking investors were actually tracking during the acute phase: the question being priced was not "how bad was today?" but "how severe is the pandemic's total trajectory, and where is it going?" — a question whose answer accumulates rather than resets each day. The dominance of the cumulative transform in the Initial Shock top rankings — present in nine of ten rows across both levels and percentage-change analyses — is the candidate selection framework's empirical confirmation that equity markets were responding to trajectory accumulation rather than daily fluctuation as the operative pandemic severity signal.

The cumulative transform's value diminishes in the Recovery phase for reasons that are equally specific. By late 2021, cumulative COVID totals had grown to such large absolute values that daily increments represented an ever-smaller percentage change, compressing the transform's variance and reducing its ability to differentiate between periods of meaningful pandemic severity change. Simultaneously, the Omicron wave's extraordinary case volume dominated the cumulative count in ways that overstated economic disruption relative to prior waves — a vaccinated population generating record case counts was not experiencing the same per-case economic damage as an unvaccinated population at lower case counts, making the cumulative total a progressively less reliable proxy for accumulated economic harm. The Recovery phase's return of `covid_c&d` without exclusive reliance on the cumulative form reflects this saturation: markets needed the directional rate-of-change signal rather than the accumulated level to distinguish economically meaningful pandemic developments from cumulative count noise.

**The 7-Day Rolling Average.** The rolling average transform (`x_roll`) consistently adds value when applied alongside cumulation, but the mechanism through which it improves scores differs from the cumulative transform's contribution and operates at a different analytical level. The rolling average's primary function is noise suppression rather than signal amplification: it removes the within-week periodicity that contaminates raw daily COVID reporting — the systematic suppression of reported counts on weekends due to reduced laboratory and administrative staffing, followed by the Monday correction spike as accumulated weekend deaths and cases are reported simultaneously — which would otherwise generate spurious short-run correlations between the reporting cycle and daily market movements. A Granger test applied to an unsmoothed daily COVID series that contains a strong weekly mechanical cycle risks detecting the reporting periodicity rather than the underlying pandemic signal; the 7-day rolling average eliminates the cycle by design, ensuring that detected predictive content reflects genuine pandemic dynamics rather than the administrative calendar.

The rolling average's value is greatest during the Initial Shock and early Adaptation phases — the periods when daily COVID reporting was subject to the largest absolute reporting noise — for a compounding reason that goes beyond noise reduction. During these phases, investors and policymakers were themselves applying implicit smoothing when interpreting pandemic data: weekly case and death averages, rather than raw daily counts, were the primary statistics featured in media coverage, policy briefings, and institutional research during 2020. The 7-day rolling average in the project's analytical framework is therefore not only reducing noise but approximating the specific representation of the COVID signal that was actually reaching and organizing market participants' assessments of pandemic severity. The dominance of rolling-plus-cumulative specifications across Initial Shock and Adaptation top rankings — with both transforms applied simultaneously in seven of ten rows during the Initial Shock — reflects this dual function: the rolling average produces the signal representation that market participants were using, and the cumulative transform captures the trajectory dimension they were tracking.

The rolling average's contribution diminishes during the Recovery phase for a reason that complements the cumulative transform's saturation problem: Omicron's case surge was so large and so rapid that the 7-day smoothing window introduced a meaningful lag at precisely the moment when the pace of change — accelerating sharply in December 2021 and decelerating sharply in February 2022 — was itself the primary information content. For commodity markets responding to the China zero-COVID lockdown mechanism, in which case threshold crossings triggered near-instantaneous policy responses, the unsmoothed daily case count was more timely and therefore more predictively relevant than the 7-day average that lagged the threshold crossing by several days. The Recovery phase's partial retreat from rolling average dominance — present in five of ten rows rather than the Adaptation phase's eight — reflects this timing consideration rather than any general deterioration in the transform's analytical value.

**The Deliberate Absence of Smoothing in the Full-Period Analysis.** The full-period exchange rate analysis applies no smoothing and no cumulation to the COVID deaths series — a configuration that stands apart from every other optimal pairing in the project and whose selection by the candidate selection framework carries its own interpretive weight. The raw daily death percentage change is the noisiest available representation of the COVID signal: it retains all of the within-week reporting periodicity, all of the day-to-day administrative volatility, and none of the trajectory information that the cumulative transform was designed to capture. That this series outperforms every smoothed, cumulated, and combined alternative across the full 966-day window when tested against exchange rate returns is evidence of a specific market property that no transformation-based analysis can directly reveal: currency markets were processing COVID mortality data at the daily reporting frequency with sufficient speed and institutional sophistication that smoothing the signal introduced lag rather than clarity.

The implication is precise. For equity markets during the Initial Shock, smoothing added value because institutional participants needed the weekly trajectory to organize their pricing decisions — the raw daily count was too noisy to act on directly. For commodity markets during the Adaptation and Recovery phases, smoothing added value because supply chain disruption effects accumulated over weekly cycles rather than responding to individual daily data points. For currency markets across the full 966-day window, smoothing subtracted value because currency market participants — operating across 24-hour global markets with deep institutional infrastructure for real-time pandemic data monitoring — had already performed the smoothing function internally. The raw daily death change was the marginal new information that moved exchange rate prices; the smoothed trajectory had already been priced before the smoothed series updated. The full-period analysis's no-smoothing configuration is therefore not a methodological aberration but the analytical system's empirical recovery of a market-specific information processing speed that distinguished currency markets from every other asset class in the project.

The evidential weight of the full-period result is correspondingly greater than the smoothed-series results from other phases can claim. A composite score of 51.81, achieved using the noisiest form of the COVID signal against the most efficiently priced and continuously traded asset class in global financial markets, across a 966-day window that explicitly averages across the pandemic's highest-signal and lowest-signal sub-periods simultaneously, represents a more demanding and more credible causal demonstration than any of the phase-specific smoothed-series results. The signal survived conditions specifically designed — by the noise characteristics of the raw series, the efficiency of currency markets, and the averaging effect of full-period pooling — to suppress rather than amplify it. That survival is the transformation analysis's most important terminal finding: COVID's causal footprint on financial markets was robust enough to remain detectable in its least favorable analytical configuration, confirming that the project's smoothed-series phase results are not artifacts of signal amplification through transformation but conservative estimates of a genuine underlying causal structure.

## 5. Conclusion

### Did COVID-19 Have a Measurable Economic Impact?

The answer is unambiguously yes, and the evidence is specific, quantified, and methodologically rigorous across five independent analytical frameworks applied consistently across four pandemic phases and six indicator categories.

The Initial Shock phase provides the most decisive demonstration. The 7-day rolling average of cumulative COVID cases and deaths Granger-caused stock market index movements across all five major equity indices during the 137-day crash and recovery window — with full four-test agreement in every significant result, directional asymmetry confirmed in both directions for all five indices, and a composite score of 66.06 that represents the project's upper bound for COVID causal dominance over financial market pricing. The S&P 500's FEVD X-share of 48.7% — rising steadily and still accumulating at the 30-period IRF horizon — is the project's single most direct quantitative statement that COVID functioned as a near-dominant driver of equity market variance during the acute shock period, explaining nearly half of the index's total forecast error variance at a horizon where its own momentum explained the other half. The lag correlation analysis confirmed that this relationship was directional rather than contemporaneous: COVID data preceded equity market movements at lags of one to five trading days depending on index composition, establishing temporal ordering that mere co-movement cannot produce.

But the more important and less obvious finding is what came after the Initial Shock — and specifically, how much COVID causal influence survived the transition from acute crisis to endemic background condition across phases and indicator classes that could not have been predicted from the Initial Shock results alone.

The Adaptation phase established that COVID's causal footprint survived the transition but reorganized itself completely. The dominant indicator class shifted from equities to metals; the dominant COVID variable shifted from the combined cases-and-deaths series to deaths alone; the dominant mechanism shifted from risk sentiment to physical supply chain disruption; and the temporal structure of the relationship shifted from broad-based, five-for-five directional causality to selective, three-for-five metal-specific Granger significance at lag 1. The composite score of 56.60 — 9.46 points below the Initial Shock but still the second-highest in the project — quantifies the attenuation while the cross-metal heterogeneity of the results specifies its structure: silver, platinum, and copper retained genuine short-run COVID causal sensitivity through the supply disruption channel; gold and palladium did not, for economically coherent reasons that map onto each metal's specific supply chain geography and demand driver composition. The Adaptation phase's most important contribution to the project's findings is not the attenuation it documents but the selectivity it reveals — a pandemic that had lost its capacity to move all markets simultaneously had retained a precisely specified capacity to move the markets whose physical supply chains remained operationally connected to pandemic severity conditions.

The Recovery phase established that this selective causal residual proved durable across a second structural transition. The composite score of 55.67 — only 0.93 points below the Adaptation figure across 292 additional days of data — confirmed a near-plateau rather than a continued monotonic decay, establishing that COVID's causal footprint on metal commodity markets had found a structural floor rather than continuing toward zero. The mechanism had shifted again — from mortality-driven mine disruption to the China zero-COVID lockdown channel operating through case-count thresholds — and the temporal structure had lengthened, with Granger significance moving from lag 1 during Adaptation to lags 2 and 4 during Recovery, reflecting the slower multi-day institutional response sequence through which Chinese lockdown triggers translated into global commodity supply and demand disruptions. The VECM framework's uniformly positive impulse responses across all five metals, despite contemporaneous Spearman correlations that were uniformly negligible, established the Recovery phase's defining analytical signature: an absent same-day relationship coexisting with genuine lagged causal structure, detectable only by frameworks that look beyond the zero-lag window.

The full-period analysis completed the causal arc by identifying exchange rates as the most temporally persistent COVID-sensitive indicator class across the pandemic's complete 966-day duration. The COVID deaths percentage change — raw, unsmoothed, and non-cumulated — Granger-caused exchange rate returns at a composite score of 51.81 that led all other indicator categories in the full-period percentage-change rankings despite using the noisiest available form of the pandemic signal against the most efficiently priced asset class in global financial markets. This result is the project's most demanding causal demonstration: surviving the signal degradation of raw daily reporting noise, the averaging attenuation of three structurally distinct causal regimes pooled into a single regression, and the market efficiency of a 24-hour globally integrated currency market simultaneously. The monetary policy divergence channel — through which differential pandemic mortality across currency areas continuously updated interest rate differential expectations and therefore exchange rate valuations — proved more durable than the sentiment channel that organized the Initial Shock and the supply disruption channel that organized the Adaptation and Recovery phases, because it operated at a temporal frequency too slow to be fully priced away within any single-phase window and too structurally embedded in central bank reaction functions to close as long as pandemic outcomes remained differentiated across major currency areas.

### What the Evidence Establishes, Precisely

Three claims follow from the project's full analytical record with sufficient cross-method and cross-phase consistency to be stated without qualification.

First, COVID-19 exerted genuine, directionally asymmetric, statistically robust causal pressure on financial markets that operated through distinct mechanisms across the pandemic's phases rather than through a single uniform channel. The causal relationship was not a contemporaneous correlation inflated by shared trend structure — it survived differencing, percentage-change transformation, Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, and Granger causality frameworks that explicitly controlled for each indicator's own return history before testing for COVID predictive content. The evidence for directionality — COVID data leading market movements rather than following them — is confirmed across multiple independent methods: Granger temporal ordering, positive-lag structure in bivariate lag correlation profiles, and VECM impulse responses that propagate COVID shocks forward through system dynamics rather than backward.

Second, the causal relationship decayed systematically but did not decay to zero. The composite score progression from 66.06 to 56.60 to 55.67 to 51.81 traces a specific attenuation arc: a large initial drop as acute uncertainty normalized, followed by a near-plateau as the supply disruption and monetary policy divergence channels sustained a structural residual that market habituation could not eliminate, followed by a full-period average that remained detectable and category-leading despite aggregating across the pandemic's highest-signal and lowest-signal sub-periods simultaneously. The floor of that residual — reflected in the mid-50s composite scores of the Adaptation and Recovery phases — represents the minimum persistent causal influence of pandemic dynamics on physical commodity supply chains and monetary policy divergence that could not be priced away as a known background condition, because the specific mechanism was continuously generating new information rather than merely confirming a static pricing framework.

Third, the form of the causal relationship changed across phases in a structured and economically interpretable sequence. The indicator class rotation from equities to metals to exchange rates tracks the pandemic's transmission mechanism progression from demand shock through sentiment, to supply shock through physical disruption, to monetary policy shock through differential recovery trajectories — each channel dominating during the phase whose temporal scale matched its transmission speed, each closing or being displaced as the pandemic evolved past the conditions that sustained it. The COVID variable rotation from combined cases-and-deaths to deaths alone to combined again — and finally to raw unsmoothed deaths for the full period — tracks the evolving informational structure of the pandemic data itself, as testing expansion, vaccine rollout, and variant-specific case-mortality decoupling changed which dimension of the pandemic signal carried the most economically relevant information for each indicator class at each point in the timeline.

### What the Evidence Does Not Establish

Precision about what the project does not claim is as important as precision about what it does. Granger causality establishes predictive precedence — that lagged COVID data contains information about subsequent financial market movements beyond what those markets' own histories contain — but it does not establish structural causation in the sense of identifying the specific institutional, behavioral, or financial mechanism through which pandemic mortality data reached asset prices on any given trading day. The project identifies the channels — sentiment, supply disruption, monetary policy divergence — through the pattern of which indicator classes respond to which COVID variables at which lags and with what FEVD X-shares, but it does not trace the microstructural path from a daily death count release to a specific price movement in a specific market. That identification requires a different analytical architecture — high-frequency transaction data, institutional flow analysis, central bank communication parsing — that lies beyond the scope of daily-frequency econometric time series methods.

The project also does not establish that the causal relationships identified would have been exploitable in real time. Granger causality identifies ex post predictive content in historical data; it does not guarantee that a real-time trading strategy based on daily COVID data releases would have generated positive risk-adjusted returns after accounting for transaction costs, execution risk, and the competing information simultaneously available to institutional market participants. The lag structures identified — one to five trading days in the Initial Shock, two to four days in the Recovery phase — are long enough that much of the COVID signal's market impact may have been incorporated before the statistical lag window is even reached, particularly for the large-cap, institutionally dominated markets where the strongest causal relationships were found.

### The Pandemic as an Economic Forcing Event

The project's deepest finding is not any specific coefficient, Granger statistic, or composite score but the structural characterization of how a major pandemic propagates through financial markets across its full lifecycle. COVID-19 did not impact financial markets through a single mechanism that weakened uniformly over time. It impacted them through a structured sequence of transmission channels that activated, dominated, and closed in a specific order determined by each channel's operational speed, institutional reach, and resistance to market habituation — from the instantaneous risk sentiment repricing of the Initial Shock, through the week-scale supply disruption dynamics of the Adaptation and Recovery commodity markets, to the month-scale monetary policy divergence that sustained the exchange rate relationship across the full 966-day window.

This three-stage transmission structure — sentiment to supply disruption to monetary divergence — is not unique to COVID-19 and is unlikely to be. Any major pandemic that combines sufficient acute severity to generate immediate financial market stress with sufficient duration to reshape physical supply chains and central bank reaction functions should produce a similar sequence, with the specific timing, magnitude, and indicator class rotation determined by the pandemic's epidemiological profile and the institutional architecture of the financial markets it encounters. The project's analytical framework — six-component, cross-phase, multi-indicator, transformation-varying — was designed to be portable to future pandemic analyses, and the cross-phase findings documented here provide the empirical baseline against which future pandemic financial market impacts can be measured, contextualized, and interpreted with a precision that was not available at the outset of the COVID-19 economic disruption.

### Which Markets Were Most Affected?

The project identifies a clear and economically interpretable hierarchy of COVID sensitivity across asset classes — not a ranking of overall importance, but a ranking of the specific dimension of COVID's economic impact that each asset class was most structurally positioned to absorb and price.

Equities were the most acutely affected during the Initial Shock, registering the project's strongest composite scores, clearest directional Granger asymmetry, and highest FEVD X-shares across all five indices. The COVID → equity channel was large, rapid, and institutionally dominant during the crash: the S&P 500's 48.7% FEVD X-share and the five-for-five Granger confirmation across all major indices with full four-test agreement represent the upper bound of COVID causal dominance that the project's framework can measure. But the channel was also the most transient of the three. By mid-2020, equity markets had internalized pandemic risk as a priced background condition, and the marginal predictive content of daily COVID data for equity returns collapsed accordingly. The equity channel's defining properties — speed, breadth, and intensity — were inseparable from the acute informational asymmetry of the Initial Shock itself, and when that asymmetry resolved, the channel closed with it.

Metal commodities were the most persistently affected across the middle and later phases of the pandemic, with the COVID → metals relationship sustaining composite scores in the mid-50s across both the Adaptation and Recovery phases while every other indicator category's COVID sensitivity weakened or disappeared. The supply disruption mechanism that connected pandemic severity to metal prices — operating through geographically concentrated mining and processing disruptions in South America, South Africa, and eventually China — was structurally slower to materialize than the equity sentiment channel but correspondingly more resistant to market habituation. It required accumulation of pandemic conditions across production networks and logistics chains before generating detectable price effects, making it invisible to the contemporaneous correlation frameworks that confirmed the equity relationship, but reliably detectable by the Granger causality and VECM frameworks that look beyond the zero-lag window. The Recovery phase's shift from mortality-driven mine disruption to case-count-driven Chinese lockdown triggers maintained the metals channel's relevance even as the pandemic's individual severity per case declined, because the operative economic mechanism had shifted from worker mortality to worker isolation — a channel that tracked case prevalence rather than fatality rates and therefore survived Omicron's case-mortality decoupling intact.

Exchange rates were the most durably affected across the pandemic's complete timeline, leading the full-period percentage-change rankings and sustaining a detectable COVID causal signal across all 966 days using the noisiest available representation of the pandemic variable. The currency channel's durability reflects its operation through transmission mechanisms that are structurally resistant to market habituation: monetary policy divergence across currency areas was continuously updated by each new wave's differential impact on national central bank reaction functions, producing a COVID → rate differential → exchange rate pathway that could not be priced away as a known background condition because the specific form of that pathway — which central banks would delay normalization, by how much, and for how long — remained genuinely uncertain as long as pandemic outcomes remained differentiated across major currency blocs. Safe-haven premium shifts that did not fully reverse as equity markets recovered, and trade flow disruptions that accumulated into persistent current account imbalances at a quarterly frequency, added further persistence layers that operated at temporal scales too slow to close within any single-phase window. The exchange rate channel's defining property — durability across all three causal regimes — was inseparable from the slow operational frequency of its dominant mechanisms, which made it the last channel to activate at full strength and the last to close as the pandemic's economic footprint contracted.

### Broader Implications

The project establishes more than a set of phase-specific findings about which COVID variables predicted which financial market returns during which sub-periods. It establishes a methodological template for studying the causal economic impact of pandemic events — and a structural characterization of how major pandemic shocks propagate through financial markets across their full lifecycle — that extends beyond COVID-19 as a specific historical event.

The composite scoring framework — weighting Granger causality (0.30), VAR/VECM dynamics (0.25), cointegration (0.20), lag correlation (0.15), and contemporaneous correlation (0.10) across all combinations of COVID variables, transformations, and economic indicator categories — provides a principled and replicable methodology for ranking causal relationships without selecting indicator pairs on the basis of prior conviction, visual inspection, or observed contemporaneous correlation alone. The phase-based decomposition demonstrates that the appropriate COVID variable, the appropriate economic indicator, the appropriate transformation, and the appropriate causal mechanism are all phase-dependent: there is no single "COVID economic impact" to be measured at any fixed point in the pandemic timeline, but rather a succession of distinct causal relationships that evolved as the pandemic's epidemiological properties changed, as markets developed pricing frameworks for novel risks, and as policymakers deployed interventions that themselves became transmission channels connecting pandemic data to asset prices.

The causal decay arc documented across the four analytical windows — 66.06 to 56.60 to 55.67 to 51.81 — is not a finding about COVID-19 specifically but a quantitative illustration of a general property of how financial markets incorporate genuinely novel information across time. When an unprecedented risk event occurs, its initial data releases carry maximum causal leverage over asset prices because markets have no established pricing framework to anchor expectations — every new data point is foundational rather than incremental, and the informational asymmetry between what the event will ultimately prove to be and what market participants can know about it in real time is at its maximum. As data accumulates, institutional models improve, policy responses are established at known scales, and the risk is progressively internalized as a permanent background condition, the marginal causal contribution of each new data release declines toward — but does not necessarily reach — zero. The floor of that residual is determined by the structural properties of whichever transmission mechanisms prove most resistant to habituation: for COVID-19, the monetary policy divergence and supply chain disruption channels sustained a structural residual in the mid-50s composite score range across the Adaptation and Recovery phases, while the acute sentiment channel that generated the Initial Shock's near-70 score had closed entirely by mid-2020.

COVID-19 is, in this respect, an unusually complete and analytically tractable natural experiment in the economics of pandemic information transmission. It offers 966 consecutive days of high-frequency financial market data spanning the full transition from acute novel shock through endemic background condition — a continuous time series that captures the complete arc from maximum informational asymmetry to full market habituation across three structurally distinct causal regimes, without the data gaps, institutional disruptions, or market closures that have complicated the analysis of prior pandemic events. The analytical architecture developed here — six-component composite scoring, cross-phase variable selection, transformation-varying specification, and multi-method causal identification — was designed to be portable to future pandemic analyses, and the cross-phase findings documented across this project provide the empirical baseline against which future pandemic financial market impacts can be measured, contextualized, and interpreted with a methodological precision that the absence of such a baseline made impossible at the outset of the COVID-19 economic disruption itself.
